Credit: MinnPost photo by Pete Majerle Duluth’s elected leaders have been divided on many things, but Council member Wendy Durrwachter feels some sense of unity forming over the need for policy responses to Immigration and Customs Enforcement activity. “I think it’s really difficult for people to not see that the Constitution is being violated,” she said. “Everyone is starting to have people who are affected by this issue.” Because of what leaders are seeing and hearing, and because Duluthians keep pushing for local government action, Durrwachter is optimistic that her colleagues will join her in doing something. Related: County attorneys nix proposal for Minnesota sheriffs to coordinate with ICE Durrwachter and one colleague, Council member David Clanaugh, proposed a resolution calling for Gov. Tim Walz to institute a temporary eviction moratorium, the aim being to keep vulnerable tenants out of ICE’s grasp. Four of their colleagues put forth a resolution barring any city resource – be it money, personnel, facilities or property – from being used to assist or facilitate enforcement of federal civil immigration laws. Initially unsure of how to wade into jurisdictional matters involving federal law enforcement agents, local officials in Duluth and other Greater Minnesota cities are beginning to try out new strategies in the face of ICE’s persistent presence . Their efforts come as lawmakers at the state and federal levels consider ways to rein in what they see as out-of-control ICE and Border Control enforcement. Duluth City Council member Wendy Durrwachter speaks during a Jan. 26 meeting. She's feeling hopeful about her colleagues supporting city policies aimed at protecting residents from ICE. Credit: Image from screenshot The port city’s policy responses At Duluth’s City Council meeting on Jan. 26, more than 20 residents used public comment period time to talk about ICE. Speakers admonished the council at times, saying that the majority hadn’t taken citizen concerns seriously enough up to that point. They repeated a list of demands brought up during past meetings, with one speaker, Brandon Parker, saying he hoped “the repetition sticks” enough to compel action. Sign up Vocal citizens calling for action are nothing new in the chambers, Durrwachter said. Issues ranging from homelessness, Israel’s occupation of Gaza and the conveyance of park lands have generated strong feelings among residents in the not-so-distant past. While Durrwachter generally supported what residents were speaking out about in those instances, the majority wasn’t with her. In contrast, she feels the council is now shifting toward some of the public demands on ICE. “This is the first time they’ve come forth and moved the council on the issue,” she said. “It’s nice to see.” A willingness to try A similar shift is playing out in Mankato. City councils in both Duluth and Mankato paused meetings in January after crowds vocally expressed dissatisfaction over what attendees saw as a lack of urgency regarding ICE. By the time their next meetings came around later in January, the atmosphere was different. Leaders came ready to talk through possible solutions. The crowds were different as well. Duluth residents silently stood up to show support for speakers. Meanwhile, Mankato’s crowd cheered when the council moved ahead on policy proposals. Mankato’s proposed ordinances will include a ban on masks for law enforcement agents operating in the city. Council member Mike Laven brought forth the motion for city staff to create ordinances for review, which would also include requirements for law enforcement agents to wear body cameras and have clear identification of their agency on display. Mankato City Council members hold a meeting on Jan. 12. More than 300 people showed up to the meeting to demand city action against ICE. At the Council's next meeting in late January, members considered specific policies in response to ICE. Credit: MinnPost photo by Brian Arola On top of these considerations, likely to come back for further review this month, Mankato passed a resolution proposed by Laven for the city to submit an amicus brief in support of a lawsuit seeking to curb ICE actions in Minnesota cities. An erosion of trust in the federal government’s actions has people turning to local governments for answers, Laven said of the proposals. “Folks keep going down the line until they can find someone they can talk to, and we’re available on Monday nights,” he said. Laven characterized the proposals as “some things we can try” in the hopes that they alleviate fear. “As a community of leaders we’re doing what we can to, I’ll say, level set what’s going on,” he said. “The other piece of it for me is, here we are today in 2026, and if we have this in place the communities of the future can then also have this in place.” Related: ICE unrest alters Twin Cities artists, but hardly deters them Residents posed more demands to the council. City attorney Pam Whitmore responded to each request in a memo , shedding light on legal interpretations on what cities are able and unable to do right now. For instance, can the city prohibit ICE agents from using city land or property? The answer seems to be a clear-cut yes. How about a city reporting all contact between employees and ICE? Not so straightforward here. Tracking it would be difficult, and the federal government is attempting to label this type of reporting as obstruction. Could the city fund groceries or grocery delivery for workers staying home due to fear of ICE abduction? Not directly, but state statutes would allow the city to award a grant to an organization or food shelf for the work. Is it in the city’s power to institute an eviction moratorium? No, but Whitmore noted that Mankato city staff already work to avoid evictions stemming from changes in employment or income. The value of a symbolism Compared to policy proposals, Mankato’s amicus brief is more symbolic. Although the city technically can’t join a lawsuit already filed by Minneapolis and St. Paul, the brief serves as a message to the community about how elected officials feel. It also opens up the possibility of courts learning about ICE’s impacts on Mankato, because part of the amicus would include submitted examples of that nature. A member of the Rochester City Council, who proposed an amicus brief at a meeting Monday, spoke about the value of steps like this before his colleagues narrowly backed his idea. The brief brought forth by Council member Patrick Keane, will document local impacts in Rochester, and be submitted when the next opportunity arises in current or future cases against ICE-related violations in the state. Real relief will come through the courts, Keane said during the meeting. He told his colleagues that judges should hear from Greater Minnesota cities, otherwise an image is perpetuated that Operation Metro Surge is only a Minneapolis issue. Related: Money for Minnesota projects approved by Congress after shutdown delay “I think a place like Rochester, and knowing what’s going on in Austin or St. Peter, Minnesota, changes the narrative for some people,” he said. “Having Rochester data in support of federal lawsuits helps explain to the courts and to the country that this is bigger than just an urban problem with overactive protesters. It is happening everywhere in Minnesota.” After Keane’s motion passed on a 4-3 vote, Mayor Kim Norton spoke to why city leaders should take positions on issues outside of their direct control. Even if speaking out doesn’t change the federal government’s position, or even if a proposed policy can’t be implemented in the end, cities can still make a point. “I use the example of what happened with St. Paul recently on some gun legislation that they took,” she said to the Council. “They knew they couldn't implement it, but they took it to make a point, and so they had a position as a council in order to lobby their state and federal colleagues.” Support the news you rely on You rely on MinnPost to report on the things that matter to you.
