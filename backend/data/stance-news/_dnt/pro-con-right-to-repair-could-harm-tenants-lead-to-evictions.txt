The Trust Project What is this? Duluth residents have spoken clearly this year: Housing is the foundation of our community. If families, seniors, and young people cannot count on safe, stable housing then everything else — education, jobs, health, and economic development — becomes that much harder. The Duluth City Council is deeply committed to expanding housing while advancing housing justice, protecting tenant rights, and ensuring safe, livable homes for every resident. ADVERTISEMENT But when good intentions collide with legal risk, we must choose carefully. The ballot measure in Duluth in the Nov. 4 election, which supporters’ brand as the “right to repair," sounds good on the surface. But it’s a Trojan horse. It promises to fix small problems and protect our most vulnerable residents, but a closer look reveals that it would have the opposite effect, increasing rents and the non-renewal of leases, leading to housing instability, consolidating property ownership, and, ultimately, harming the very families it claims to help. Oct 28, 2025 &#183; By DyAnna Grondahl, Cody Pals, and Chloe Holloway The current campaign argues for a "common-sense tool" to support renters. But the truth is, Minnesota already has robust, common-sense tools in place. Our state laws, particularly Minnesota Statutes Chapter 504B, are the bedrock of tenant protection. They already mandate that landlords keep properties safe and habitable. Tenants already have a clear path to legal remedies, including rent escrow and emergency remedies, to ensure repairs are made promptly. These are not vague suggestions; they are enforceable laws that have been tested and proven to work. The ballot measure's promise to "fix the problems while they are small" is exactly what our current laws are designed to do. Instead of creating a duplicate, confusing, and burdensome new system, we should focus on strengthening and enforcing the protections we already have. Which the City Council did in July by requiring landlords to respond to repair requests in 14 days of notification and to provide tenants with clear information of their rights and how to communicate repair needs and how to engage city resources if problems persist. The city also funds resources to navigate this via the nonprofit One Roof Community Housing’s Tenant Landlord Connection program. A new, voter-approved ordinance would simply add another layer of bureaucracy on an overwhelmed and already stretched City Hall staff, creating more red tape for both tenants and landlords. Another unintended consequence of the proposed ballot initiative could be the increase of non-renewal of leases. A landlord may choose not to renew a lease for many reasons, and in most jurisdictions they are not required to provide a specific cause. While retaliatory non-renewals for legally protected activities are illegal, proving retaliation can be challenging for a tenant. A landlord can simply state they are taking the property off the market or making major renovations, even if the real reason is a desire to avoid a tenant who has exercised their "right to repair." ADVERTISEMENT The City Council’s alternative ordinance avoids these traps. It ensures repairs are made, improves transparency, and requires landlord accountability. It is landlords — not tenants — who own properties, collect rents, and carry the legal responsibility to maintain safe and habitable housing. Tenants should not be left shouldering that burden themselves. Voting “no” on the so-called “right-to-repair” ballot measure is not a vote against tenants. It is a vote for a thoughtful, legally sound approach that delivers real protection without creating new risks. Duluth deserves a policy that delivers justice and security for tenants, not just promises. For the protection of our 35,000 renters in Duluth, I urge you to vote “no” on Question 1 on Nov. 4. Roz Randorf is the elected representative of District 3 on the Duluth City Council. The district is in the center of Duluth, including the hillside neighborhoods, Park Point, downtown, and Observation Hill. She wrote this commentary at the request of the News Tribune Opinion page.
