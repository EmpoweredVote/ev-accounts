Sign up Ethel Kennedy died this week. But I don’t have to believe it if I don’t want to. I didn’t know Ethel Kennedy. However, our paths crossed when I was a young Foreign Service Officer in Brazil, assigned to help the advance team for her husband, Sen. Robert F. Kennedy, on his visit to Brazil. They needed someone to translate for them and help arrange for a major speech that Bobby gave at a university. Being involved with the Kennedy team juggernaut was an education. For three days, the entire public affairs section of the embassy worked overtime to accommodate the senator’s visit. Typing drafts of his speech. Duplicating it. Providing media coverage. Setting up interviews with prominent local leaders. Providing any assistance requested from anyone in the entourage, which must have included a dozen people. What I remember most vividly was translating for Kennedy’s advance person when he met with the rector of the university to set up logistics for the speech. He rejected the placement of the podium in the lecture hall. Reminding the rector of the assassination of Bobby’s brother, President John F. Kennedy, the advance person insisted that the podium had to be placed where a quick secure exit could happen if an emergency arose. The speech drew thunderous applause from the university audience, and the entire visit was a resounding media triumph. But the staff of the public affairs section gave sighs of relief when the Kennedys went on to their next destination. Ethel Kennedy graciously sent gifts of appreciation to staffers who had played key logistics roles in making the visit a success. Ethel also shows up in my recently published novel, “ Goodbye Bobby .” Three fictional characters here react to her and the Kennedy family: RFK’s special projects aide Adam Walker, the novel protagonist Charlie Parnell, and Charlie’s nine-year-old stepdaughter Angelita, who got to know RFK Jr. Adam took another swig of his beer and grinned for the first time since entering Charlie’s apartment. “Charlie, I’ve never seen anything like this. People try to shake his hand or grab a cufflink or just touch him. In the motorcades, he stands in the back of a convertible, and he’s jostled so much by the crowd, it takes four guys in the back seat to hang onto him and keep him steady.” Charlie looked askance at Adam. “Motorcade? Given what happened to his brother, I wouldn’t have anything to do with a motorcade if I were in his shoes.” “It gets hairy sometimes. In San Francisco, a firecracker went off as we were passing by. Ethel ducked to the floor and Bobby flinched. But he kept standing up, waving at the crowd.” It was midnight in Los Angeles by the time Kennedy showed up to celebrate this successful moment in his path to the White House. Just behind him, Adam Walker led Kennedy’s pregnant wife Ethel by the arm to the podium to stand next to her husband in their moment of triumph. The victory speech was a rambling bore, Charlie thought, as he watched on TV, not even coming close to the ringing rhetoric of his speeches. It was mostly a listing of those who had contributed to the campaign, from California politicos to Bobby’s wife Ethel to their dog Freckles. “So my thanks to all of you, and on to Chicago, and let’s win there.” Chants of “We want Bobby! We want Bobby!” roared through the air. He exited the ballroom through a pantry leading to the hotel kitchen. The pantry was bottlenecked with Bobby’s bodyguards and close followers. Adam trailed behind Kennedy, acting as a buffer for Ethel as they entered the crowded pantry. Bobby reached out to shake the hand of a kitchen worker The cathedral was so packed that Charlie and Angelita were pushed to the edge of their pew. Outside, an overflow crowd of mourners stood by the entrance. Legendary musician Leonard Bernstein conducted a piece of classical music, pop-singer Andy Williams sang “Battle Hymn of the Republic,” and Bobby’s brother, Senator Ted Kennedy, gave an eloquent eulogy. Then Bobby’s son, fourteen-year-old Robert Junior, stepped to the pulpit. “Oh no!” said Angelita. She turned to Charlie. “They aren’t going to make him talk, are they? That’s his dad. They can’t make him speak right now. That’s cruel.” Tears came to her eyes as she stared at the boy she had struck a bond with while beating him at chess. The boy who hadn’t yet gotten over the death of his uncle and now had to accept the murder of his father. The boy who had seemed to understand her loss of her mother. “You didn’t make me do anything like that when Mama died. Why are they doing that?” She was standing, half in the pew, half in the aisle, tears on her cheeks. Charlie pulled her back to a sitting position and put his arm around her shoulders as they watched. With a cracking voice, the boy read excerpts from his father’s speeches, then returned to his pew. The mass proceeded to its end, and Angelita watched, mesmerized as the red-capped Cardinal led the procession up the aisle toward the huge doors of the cathedral. She scrutinized the faces of the immediate family as they approached her. She tried to catch the eye of Bobby Jr. walking by his mother, Ethel, whose face was covered with a black veil. But his eyes were cast down to his shoes. The entire cathedral was silent, but for people’s steps on the marble floor. When the casket drew abreast of her, Angelita stood up, faced the aisle, and placed her right hand over her heart, her fluffy Afro, standing out. J.J. Harrigan served with the U.S. Foreign Service in Brazil, subsequently taught political science at Hamline University and currently writes historical novels. He lives in Stillwater.
