In July 2022, the Fargo School Board voted unanimously to fire Kevin Kennedy for misconduct and inappropriate behavior, according to Valley View News Live . According to the outlet’s reporting, the North Dakota district received at least six different complaints about Kennedy during a three year period. “Many of the complaints claim Kennedy had ‘crossed boundaries’ with students and created an uncomfortable learning environment,” according to a different Valley View News article . Kennedy has since moved to the Twin Cities with his wife, and this past March held auditions for Kennedy’s Cue Line Productions’ first show in the area, called “The Bakken,” which opened at the Historic Mounds Theatre earlier this month. One of the actors in the show, Nora Targonski-O’Brien, was unsettled by two comments the director made — one during the rehearsal process, and another in her dressing room at the show’s second performance — which prompted her to Google him. That’s when she read news reports about Kennedy’s past behavior, and decided to quit the show the next morning, she said. Cue Line Productions canceled the remaining performances, according to Krista Schnelle, board member of Historic Mounds Theatre. Soon the company also lost its venue for the Minnesota Fringe Festival, when Arts’ Nest, the nonprofit that runs the Phoenix Theatre, canceled the company’s contract. "Due to community concerns of current and former behavior, Arts' Nest cancelled the rental contract and returned Cue Line's deposit,” said Jenna Papke, executive director of The Arts’ Nest. Originally from the Twin Cities, Kennedy was hired at Fargo South High School as an English and drama teacher in 2013, and also directed and coached numerous plays and musicals, sketch comedy shows, and an improv troupe during his time there. He disputes the accusations against him, and has said that of the 28 pieces of evidence used by the school district, all but two were thrown out by the hearing judge. He also noted that one board member recused themselves from the hearing, and another wasn’t present. Artscape Newsletter | Subscribe for the latest news and events across all forms of the arts. He told me he was at a point where he just wanted to move on with his life. Kevin Kennedy Credit: Fargo Public Schools “Put yourself in my shoes for a second,” he said. “You’re having auditions, and you have 30 people there and say, ‘OK, before you audition, I just want everybody to know that I was accused of all these horrific things. They weren't true, they didn't happen, but I lost my job over it. OK, let's read the script now.’” I asked him about some of the accusations against him, like had he asked a student if she had ever been asked for nude photos. Kennedy told me a student had entered his room during his free period, while another student was in the room, and said, “I’m sick of boys asking for nudes,” he said. That’s when he asked the student who had been sending her nudes, and advised her to not send any of that type of photo to anyone. Another accusation that he touched a student was taken out of context, according to Kennedy. “We had a scene where this guy had to put his hands around her neck, and I was showing him what to do, and I was telling him what to do and all this stuff,” he told me. “And she said that she was extremely uncomfortable. I asked her permission to demonstrate at that time, because the other actor had to do it, and he was doing it wrong.” Kennedy told me he asked the young woman for permission to demonstrate the move, and that she had said yes. Related: Intimacy directors help create a space for theater actors to safely portray intimate scenes Since his firing, he has renewed his North Dakota teaching license, and also has applied for a Minnesota teaching license, which was granted after an investigation, though he is not currently teaching, he told me. Targonski-O’Brien saw the audition notice for “The Bakken,” which Kennedy wrote and directed, earlier this spring. The play was about a man who returns home after a period away to his hometown near the Bakken oil field. The first half of the play centers around the man and his brother and reveals long-held secrets. In the second half, the main character has a scene with his brother’s wife, with whom he has had a previous relationship when they were younger. The show appealed to Targonski-O’Brien because it was only a month-long commitment with the first rehearsals conducted over Zoom, and the performances were in a convenient location. She was also intrigued by Kennedy’s director’s note and the character description for the female role. “I did like something about the arrested development of it,” she said. “When I saw the director's note, I kind of feel like this is someone who's showing themselves at their lowest and blurting out and doing and saying the kind of their most (basic) things.” Not long into the rehearsal process, however, Targonski-O’Brien was having second thoughts about being in the show. She hadn’t gotten a contract for her role, and felt she wasn’t on the same page with the director in regard to the story they were telling and what the characters were thinking and feeling. “He would just shout over me,” she said. “I asked him to stop several times. It was just a very strange experience overall, and I ignored my gut for too long.” Then, one day in a Zoom rehearsal, Kennedy encouraged the actors to promote the show on social media. Targonski-O’Brien told him she’d limit promoting the show to avoid inviting some in her work circle, including people who were too young for the show’s adult material. According to Targonski-O’Brien, Kennedy said, "If anything, they'll come because your top comes off.” She was taken aback by the comment. “You never know how you're going to react to a comment like that. You want to think you're going to stand up and walk out and not go to rehearsal ever again, but I didn't say anything,” she said. Kennedy denied making the comment. “I absolutely did not say that,” he said. “When we were talking about who to invite, she said she didn’t have anybody. I asked about colleagues and she said they wouldn’t come. Nothing was said about students.” Then another incident proved a breaking point. For the second performance on May 9, Targonski-O’Brien had arrived late because of a work event, and had informed the stage manager. Kennedy came to her dressing room in the middle of the show. After a conversation in the dressing room, Kennedy again brought up whether the actor could invite people to the show, and brought up the middle school-age youth with whom she works. Targonski-O’Brien responded that inviting young people wouldn’t be appropriate given the adult content of the play, and also that she had a scene where she appeared wearing only a bra. “They’ll never forget to come to your class,” she told me he said. “They'll really pay attention to you.” Kennedy disputed Targonski-O’Brien’s version of events. When I talked to him, he told me that because they only had 17 people in the audience that night, he jokingly asked her to invite the youth she works with to come to the show. “She kind of looks at me angrily and says, ‘This is not an appropriate show for kids. They're middle schoolers.’ And I said I was just joking. I'm sorry. I didn't mean anything,” he said. “But whatever, it didn’t fly. So that was fine. So I just left. I was joking about the kids coming, because the show is not meant for middle school audiences.” I emailed several people involved with the show, but they either didn’t respond or said they wouldn’t speak to me on the record. After their conversation on May 9, Targonski-O’Brien went on stage and performed in the second act of the play. When she got home, she looked online and discovered the news reports about Kennedy’s firing in Fargo. The next morning, she talked with a friend and after that decided to quit the production. The next day, Targonski-O’Brien posted about her experience on a Facebook group for the Twin Cities theater community. “It has come to my attention that a confirmed predator is attempting to hide within our TC theater community,” she wrote. Kennedy responded to the post, which is a different approach from what he did in Fargo. When he was suspended without pay in April 2022 and fired several months later, he was advised by his lawyer to not say anything. “I just had to bite my tongue, because I got fired in March, and I had to wait till my hearing in June,” he said. “There was kind of an explosion in March, and then it kind of died down, and then there was reignited in June in the papers and stuff like that.” This time, he said he wanted to give his side of the story. “I was like, I have to respond,” he said. “So that's why I wrote that long Facebook response, and I've had some people reach out to me, but you know, it doesn't matter what you say. People are going to believe what they want to believe, there's nothing you can do about that.” In spite of what happened, Kennedy wants to continue writing and producing shows, though he doesn’t plan to teach anymore. “I think those days are done,” he said. “I just, I don't know that I can go back into that.” SUPPORT MINNPOST Artscape stories like this one come to you each week to highlight the arts and culture happenings around Minnesota.
