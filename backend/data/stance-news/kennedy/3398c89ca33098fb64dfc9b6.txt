Sign up Back in 1991, when the Twins were storming their way to the World Series and “The Silence of the Lambs” ruled the box office, rookie state representative Becky Lourey got to know Erin Murphy, a nurse just starting advocacy work for the National Nurses Association. State Sen. Erin Murphy, DFL-St. Paul “I was a baby organizer,” said Murphy, speaking from her capacious office as the current Minnesota Senate Majority Leader. After a day at the Capitol, Murphy and Lourey would drive into Minneapolis and share a meal at a 24-hour Perkins on Franklin Avenue just off I-94. “We worked long hours. So, it was important they were open all night,” said Lourey, a DFLer from Kerrick, which is located north of the Twin Cities. What Lourey and Murphy were hammering out was MinnesotaCare, a bill to dramatically grow government-funded health care in Minnesota through inventing new revenue streams and tapping federal Medicaid dollars. Becky Lourey “It was a nation-leading program,” Murphy said. “It was ambitious and important to say, ‘If you’re not getting health care through work, we want you to have coverage.’” For decades, Minnesota has been a national leader in helping its residents access health insurance. As of 2023, 4% of Minnesotans did not have health insurance compared to 8% nationally, according to the Kaiser Family Foundation. In providing health care, Minnesota has banked on Medicaid each step of the way. “Minnesota has always tried to expand coverage to as many people as possible,” said Rep. Tina Liebling, DFL-Rochester. “We always try to maximize federal dollars.” But the state squeezing all it can from Medicaid dovetails with spiraling costs in long-term care. It also makes Minnesota exceedingly vulnerable if Donald Trump and a GOP-controlled Congress really put Medicaid dollars on the chopping block. Over 1.2 million Minnesotans, more than a fifth of the state’s population, receive Medical Assistance, the state’s name for its Medicaid program, according to the Department of Human Services (DHS), which runs the program. (Another 105,000 people are on the current version of MinnesotaCare , where enrollees can access federal tax credits.) The feds pay between 51% to 90% of the health care costs for each Minnesotan on Medical Assistance. In recent years, Washington has typically paid for 60% of the state’s overall Medical Assistance budget . A fiercely debated U.S. House budget resolution would wipe out $880 billion in Medicaid spending over the next 10 years. According to Kaiser , if that cut really becomes law it would slash Minnesota Medicaid money by $2 billion yearly, or around 18% of the feds' contribution. If that cut happens, Murphy said, “the system is going to wither.” Republicans mostly agree. Sen. Jim Abeler, R-Anoka, described Medical Assistance as a “core mission” of the Minnesota government. Rep. Nolan West, R-Blaine, expressed disbelief that the federal cuts will really transpire. “I don’t think the feds are going to cut anything that’s not a COVID program. I think I would put money on it if you’re interested.” Maybe West is right. After all, some members of Congress are entertaining second thoughts . So, instead of looking at what might happen, here is a primer on how Minnesota got here. How Minnesota operates Medicaid In the annals of great Minnesota statespeople, Gov. Karl Rolvaag gets nary a mention. Gov. Karl Rolvaag Credit: Minnesota Historical Society A DFLer, Rolvaag found himself in constant gridlock with legislative Republicans. When he was voted out of office in 1966 after serving one term, President Lyndon Johnson named Rolvaag U.S. ambassador to Iceland. However! It was Rolvaag who signed into law the creation of Medical Assistance in 1966, one year after Lyndon Johnson and congressional Democrats revolutionized American health care by enacting Medicare and Medicaid. Back in the mid-60s, it was Medicare, guaranteeing health care for every single American 65 years old and over, that got the lion’s share of attention. Former President Harry Truman (sitting) and Vice President Hubert Humphrey (standing, center) look on as President Lyndon B. Johnson signs the Medicare Bill in Independence, Missouri on July 30, 1965. Credit: Associated Press “Medicaid was almost an afterthought,” said Mary Kennedy, who served as Minnesota Medicaid director from 1997 to 2004. But it was (and is) Medicaid that not only guaranteed health care for children, the blind, and people with disabilities but also backstopped long-term care services if (and usually when) Medicare money runs out. “Medicare only covers a little bit of respite care,” said Jan Malcolm, Department of Health commissioner under the Jesse Ventura, Mark Dayton and Tim Walz administrations. “Medicaid is what is relied upon for chronic illness.” In 2024, Minnesota spent about $7.2 billion on long-term care for the chronically ill and disabled, providing these recipients care at either a residential facility (read: nursing home) or in their own abode. This is, by a mile, the priciest part of Medical Assistance. Home care for Medicaid patients is the result of the mind-bendingly bureaucratic sounding federal Medicaid waiver program, which Congress passed in 1981 to give states additional flexibility in how they utilized Medicaid money, either in terms of new enrollees or how services were provided. What this means is that if a state is proactive enough to endure a tedious application and auditing process, they can get more Medicaid dollars, enjoy more leeway in how they spend the money they have, or (oftentimes) both. Minnesota loves Medicaid waivers. Its first waiver came in 1981 when Republican Gov. Al Quie got federal permission to shift long-term care from hospitals to smaller community clinics or even a patient’s own home. Two years later, Minnesota nabbed another waiver, this one to experiment with managed care, which completely changed how Medical Assistance operates and is still what the state uses today. The way managed care works is that DHS contracts with health care plans. Today, these include UCare, Blue Cross Blue Shield, and Hennepin Health, among others . Daily Newsletter | MinnPost's top stories delivered to your inbox every weekday. Sign up Each month, DHS sends a payment to the health plans based on the plan’s number of enrollees. (Medical Assistance recipients typically have a couple of choices for a managed care plan, depending on where they live.) The health plan then works between the enrollees and health care providers to deliver medical checkups, procedures and long-term care strategies within a budget not exceeding the monthly payments. “The hope was to manage the costs as well as coordinate the care in a better way,” said Julie Brunner, an assistant commissioner at the Department of Human Services in the 1980s. Most states eventually jumped on the managed care bandwagon, working with health care plans that profit from the difference between these monthly payments and the cost of care. Minnesota, however, has distinguished itself by partnering with managed care organizations that are either nonprofits, or owned by a county government. (The for-profit, Minnetonka-based UnitedHealth Group was briefly a managed care organization before DHS ended its relationship with the company last year.) “The arrangements kept in place the insurers,” said Sheila Kiscaden, a former Republican and later Independent state senator from Rochester. “We were not favorable to for-profit companies.” State Sen. Sheila Kiscaden testifying before the Health and Human Services Committee in the early '90s. Credit: Photo courtesy of the Minnesota Senate Part of why Medical Assistance traditionally gets bipartisan support, Kiscaden said, is that Republicans see managed care as controlling costs. State Rep. Tina Liebling But managed care has always had critics on the left, as well as some doctors. Each group can view insurers as meddling middlemen. Liebling, who moved to Rochester when her husband took a job as a Mayo Clinic physician, has brought both of these perspectives to her two decades as a legislator. “Private insurance companies stand in between provider and patient,” Liebling said. “They don’t really manage care. They manage payment. Being nonprofit just means they are tax exempt. It doesn’t tell you if they are really acting in the public interest.” Before the Affordable Care Act, there was MinnesotaCare In 1992, the social programs that bloomed under the Lyndon Johnson (and, I suppose, Karl Rolvaag) administration were in a state of upheaval. Bill Clinton was elected president as a centrist Democrat open to work requirements for government aid, and campaigning to “end welfare as we know it,” something he later accomplished with a GOP-led Congress. Ross Perot was the most successful third-party presidential candidate in 80 years, warning voters about runaway spending leading to unspeakably bad federal deficits, which he likened to “a crazy aunt in the basement.” Ah, but then there was Minnesota. During that year’s legislative session, Republican (yes, Republican) Gov. Arne Carson signed into law MinnesotaCare, a measure in which the state government pledged to help pay for the health care of every single Minnesotan living at or below 200% of the federal poverty level. Related from the MinnPost archive: How 1990s bipartisanship delivered health care reform in Minnesota The law made national news, The New York Times reporting , “About 30 states are studying legislation to provide health insurance to those who do not have it. But Minnesota has come closer to enacting a sweeping measure than any other state in the three years since Oregon created a plan to give coverage to all residents, in part by expanding eligibility for Medicaid.” Speaking about MinnesotaCare today, Carlson said he thought the investment could lead to healthier Minnesotans, and thereby a decreased strain on emergency medical services. Former Gov. Arne Carlson Credit: MinnPost file photo by James Nord He also did not want any “important” legislation “to crash on the partisan rocks.” “I was independent of the GOP party leadership since I ran against the Republican Party’s endorsed candidate and was considered to be too liberal overall,” Carlson emailed while vacationing in Barcelona. “This environment meant that I was free to govern from the middle but lacked a strong party base in the Legislature. This led to building majority coalitions on every contentious issue. And it worked.” Murphy’s recollection is that Carlson and legislative Republicans finally went along with MinnesotaCare because it outlined how to pay for expanded health care. This included a 2% tax on the revenue of health care providers, a funding mechanism now used by most states. “Minnesota was one of the first states in the nation to rely on a provider tax,” Murphy said. (MinnesotaCare’s other revenue stream? A tax hike on cigarettes for the princely sum of 5 cents a pack. It is now 79 cents per pack.) Childless adults were a population federal Medicaid would not touch until the 2010 Affordable Care Act. But in 1995, the Clinton administration granted Minnesota a federal waiver to fund parts of MinnesotaCare, including expanding the definition of a caretaker and streamlining benefits for pregnant women. MinnesotaCare ushered in an era of optimism. In 1997, the ad hoc Minnesota Health Care Commission released a survey finding that 90% of state residents were either “very satisfied” or “extremely satisfied” with their health care plan. Kiscaden, who spoke on the phone from her living room overlooking a Mayo Clinic hospital, said she takes pride in MinnesotaCare. “I was supportive because I represented a place that calls itself America’s city for health.” The Affordable Care Act fork in the road It was a mere decade and a half ago that the nation, or at least readers of political blogs , was totally obsessed with health care policy. In 2010, President Barack Obama signed into law the Affordable Care Act, which created health insurance exchanges, outlawed insurers rejecting patients because of pre-existing conditions, and let children stay on their parents’ health care up until age 26. Vice President Kamala Harris stands with former President Barack Obama during an event hosted by U.S. President Joe Biden on the Affordable Care Act, Obama's top legislative accomplishment, in the East Room at the White House in Washington, U.S., April 5, 2022. REUTERS/Leah Millis Almost lost in the shuffle was an ACA provision that expanded Medicaid eligibility to anyone at or below 138% of the federal poverty line. It was the first time adults without a child or a disability qualified for Medicaid. It was also the first time the federal government would foot the entire bill (today the feds fund 90% of the cost for this population segment). Once again, Minnesota was poised to leverage federal Medicaid money. Except the politics of Medicaid had changed. Not wanting to endorse anything associated with Obama, Republican Gov. Tim Pawlenty and the Republican-controlled Legislature rejected the ACA Medicaid expansion. Abeler, a state representative at the time, said he and his fellow Republicans were worried that by insuring able-bodied adults, Minnesota was “moving away from the people who had genuine needs.” State Sen. Jim Abeler, R-Anoka, sits in his office at the Capitol during the 2025 legislative session. He was first elected (to the House first) in 1998 and has been among the key lawmakers shaping the state's health care system. Credit: MinnPost photo by Matthew Blake During the 2010 legislative session, DFLers and Republicans struck a deal. If the Republican candidate for governor, Tom Emmer, won, they would stop their push for Medicaid expansion. But if Mark Dayton, the DFL candidate, won, he could sign an executive order accepting the Medicaid expansion. Dayton won, leading to one of the liveliest signing ceremonies in Minnesota political history. Gov. Mark Dayton signs an executive order to expand Medicaid on Jan. 5, 2011. Credit: MinnPost file photo by James Nord “The new governor ended up with a standing-room-only audience of both protesters and supporters who crowded into the governor’s reception room, " MinnPost reported at the time. “They clogged the doorway and even spilled into the hall.” “It was a gamble that paid off,” Murphy said of the 2010 deal. “There was no guarantee who was going to win.” But the Republican-controlled legislature had second thoughts, balking at the executive order (and also killing any legislation to set up a health insurance exchange.) Protesters were among those who flooded Gov. Mark Dayton's reception room when he signed an executive order to expand Medicaid as part of the ACA. Credit: MinnPost file photo by James Nord With the state Legislature winding down its 2012 session, The New York Times reported , “The federal divide over health care is reflected at the state level, even in Minnesota, which has a progressive tradition and a long history of innovation and bipartisan cooperation on health policy.” Once again, the Legislature punted until the election. And, once again, DFLers won, this time wresting control of the House and Senate in the November 2012 elections. In February 2013, Dayton signed a bill legislatively codifying the Medicaid expansion. A month after signing a bill to expand Medicaid through the federal Affordable Care Act, DFL Gov. Mark Dayton signed the health insurance exchange bill into law, creating MNsure, on March 20, 2013. At his left is state Sen. Tony Lourey and at right is state Rep. Joe Atkins. Credit: MinnPost file photo by Tom Olmsheid While all this was happening, DFLers were also trying to preserve MinnesotaCare. They lobbied Washington to keep this population on existing health plans, while shifting the cost to the federal government. They also wanted to keep MinnesotaCare around for those making 139% to 200% of the federal poverty level. Tony Lourey, son of Becky Lourey and a DFL state senator from Kerrick at that time, recalls flights to Washington where he implored former Sen. Al Franken to give Minnesota another federal waiver. “It was tricky getting the feds to grant our waiver,” Tony Lourey recalled. “They were pretty inundated with requests.” On New Years Day 2015, a revised federal waiver kicked in , giving subsidies to MinnesotaCare enrollees who earned too much to get on Medicaid. The DFL political triumphs in the Dayton era had major consequences for Minnesotans and health care providers. Between 2013 and 2023, the number of state residents on Medical Assistance climbed more than 80% to nearly 1.4 million (it has decreased to 1.2 million since then). Medicaid under the microscope Medical Assistance has perennially been accompanied by audits showing that a hospital or treatment center or doctor’s office overbilled DHS or underbilled DHS, or that DHS got too much or too little money from the U.S. Department of Human Services. Generally, this has been seen as the cost of operating a sprawling program serving a vulnerable population. “It is just the nature of these programs,” Murphy said. “There are going to be people who choose to do the wrong thing.” Senate Majority Leader Erin Murphy, DFL-St. Paul, arrives for budget negotiations carrying a jar of pickles (a healthy snack, she said) and a budget binder, Monday, May 5, at the Capitol. Back when she and former state Sen. Becky Lourey were working on MinnesotaCare in 1991, the now-closed Perkins in Minneapolis fueled long hours. Credit: MinnPost photo by Tom Olmsheid But the clamor over waste or even fraud in Medical Assistance has played at a higher volume in the Walz administration. In 2019, the governor’s first year in office, an Office of the Legislative Auditor report found that Medical Assistance owed the federal government $48 million after it overpaid substance abuse treatment providers. The audit coincided with Tony Lourey resigning as DHS commissioner just six months into the job. A month after Lourey left, Deputy DHS Commissioner Claire Wilson departed as did Marie Zimmerman, an assistant commissioner who focused on Medical Assistance oversight. Then-DHS Commissioner Tony Lourey speaks at legislative hearing in March 2019. Credit: MinnPost photo by Peter Callaghan Republicans pounced. "I think we have moved from a department in disarray to a department in distress," then-Sen. Scott Jensen, R-Chaska, told the Star Tribune at the time. Walz named Jodi Harpstead the new DHS head, and she stayed on until this January. When Harpstead resigned, the Senate Republican caucus fired off a press release of bad-sounding news during her tenure with the headline, “DHS commissioner resigns amid fraud scandals.” For instance, the release noted there was a December 2024 Office of Legislative Auditor report that discovered DHS “did not comply with significant-finance legal requirements we tested and generally had inadequate internal controls.” The audit found that DHS has not attempted to recover $40 million in overpayments to Medical Assistance providers. That’s a lot of wasted money. But the time period that the auditor looked at spanned between 2006 and 2023. In the year 2023 alone, the Medical Assistance budget stood at $18.1 billion. The Medical Assistance budget has been at least $10 billion every single year since 2015. DHS’s failure to recover a little over $2 million a year in provider overpayments is the equivalent of a millionaire forgetting to cash a $200 check. Still, there has been a bipartisan push to go after Medical Assistance fraud, with possible legislation including hiring more people to investigate provider claims. Republicans are also pushing to remove undocumented immigrants from MinnesotaCare, though payments represent just a small part of the program. The real elephant in the room: costs for people who require continual, long-term care. In his State of the State address this month, Walz alluded to these patients. Gov. Tim Walz presents his State of the State address to a joint session of the legislature in the House Chamber the State Capitol, Wed., April 23, 2025. Credit: MinnPost photo by Tom Olmscheid “Right now, we’re the most generous state in the nation when it comes to these programs and services – and we should be proud of that,” Walz said. Walz wants to cap this generosity a bit, noting that long-term care waivers will soon eat up one-eighth of the entire state budget. The DHS budget bill, which passed the House floor on Monday, is perhaps a start. It proposes $427 million in long-term care waiver spending reductions . What neither the governor nor the Legislature have put forward is a contingency plan for significant federal Medicaid cuts. “There is no way the state of Minnesota can come close to backfilling what is lost,” Walz said in April at the Martin Luther Campus, an assisted living facility in Bloomington, Minnesota, whose primary revenue source is Medicaid reimbursements. “We’re preparing for the worst-case scenario but it’s almost impossible.” MinnPost's in-depth, state government coverage goes beyond the legislative session. Al Quie got federal permission to shift long-term care from hospitals to smaller community clinics or even a patient’s own home.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Two years later, Minnesota nabbed another waiver, this one to experiment with managed care, which completely changed how Medical Assistance operates and is still what the state uses today.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The way managed care works is that DHS contracts with health care plans. Today, these include UCare, Blue Cross Blue Shield, and Hennepin Health, &lt;a href="https://mn.gov/dhs/partners-and-providers/contact-us/minnesota-health-care-programs/providers/mcos.jsp"&gt;among others&lt;/a&gt;.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Each month, DHS sends a payment to the health plans based on the plan’s number of enrollees. (Medical Assistance recipients typically have a couple of choices for a managed care plan, depending on where they live.)&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The health plan then works between the enrollees and health care providers to deliver medical checkups, procedures and long-term care strategies within a budget not exceeding the monthly payments.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“The hope was to manage the costs as well as coordinate the care in a better way,” said Julie Brunner, an assistant commissioner at the Department of Human Services in the 1980s.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Most states eventually jumped on the managed care bandwagon, working with health care plans that profit from the difference between these monthly payments and the cost of care.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Minnesota, however, has &lt;a href="https://ccf.georgetown.edu/2024/02/06/minnesota-medicaid-revisits-the-question-managed-care-or-fee-for-service/"&gt;distinguished itself&lt;/a&gt; by partnering with managed care organizations that are either nonprofits, or owned by a county government. (The for-profit, Minnetonka-based UnitedHealth Group was briefly a managed care organization before DHS &lt;a href="https://www.startribune.com/unitedhealthcare-loses-appeal-to-retain-minnesota-medicaid-business-in-2025/601174915"&gt;ended its relationship&lt;/a&gt; with the company last year.)&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“The arrangements kept in place the insurers,” said Sheila Kiscaden, a former Republican and later Independent state senator from Rochester. “We were not favorable to for-profit companies.”&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198565) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;Part of why Medical Assistance traditionally gets bipartisan support, Kiscaden said, is that Republicans see managed care as controlling costs.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (403251) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;But managed care has always had critics on the left, as well as some doctors. Each group can view insurers as meddling middlemen. Liebling, who moved to Rochester when her husband took a job as a Mayo Clinic physician, has brought both of these perspectives to her two decades as a legislator.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“Private insurance companies stand in between provider and patient,” Liebling said. “They don’t really manage care. They manage payment. Being nonprofit just means they are tax exempt. It doesn’t tell you if they are really acting in the public interest.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;h3 class="wp-block-heading"&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Before the Affordable Care Act, there was MinnesotaCare&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/h3&gt; &lt;p&gt;In 1992, the social programs that bloomed under the Lyndon Johnson (and, I suppose, Karl Rolvaag) administration were in a state of upheaval.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Bill Clinton was elected president as a centrist Democrat open to work requirements for government aid, and &lt;a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/video/politics/bill-clinton-in-1992-ad-a-plan-to-end-welfare-as-we-know-it/2016/08/30/9e6350f8-6ee0-11e6-993f-73c693a89820_video.html"&gt;campaigning&lt;/a&gt; to “end welfare as we know it,” something he &lt;a href="https://www.nytimes.com/1996/08/23/us/clinton-signs-bill-cutting-welfare-states-in-new-role.html"&gt;later accomplished&lt;/a&gt; with a GOP-led Congress.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Ross Perot was the most successful third-party presidential candidate in 80 years, warning voters about runaway spending leading to unspeakably bad federal deficits, which &lt;a href="https://www.youtube.com/shorts/2x1iw858p7M"&gt;he likened&lt;/a&gt; to “a crazy aunt in the basement.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Ah, but then there was Minnesota.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;During that year’s legislative session, Republican (yes, Republican) Gov. Arne Carson signed into law MinnesotaCare, a measure in which the state government pledged to help pay for the health care of every single Minnesotan living at or below 200% of the federal poverty level.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;h4 class="wp-block-heading has-secondary-color has-text-color has-link-color"&gt;Related from the MinnPost archive: &lt;a href="https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2009/07/bipartisanship-1990s-delivered-health-care-reform-minnesota/"&gt;How 1990s bipartisanship delivered health care reform in Minnesota&lt;/a&gt; &lt;/h4&gt; &lt;p&gt;The law made national news, The New York Times &lt;a href="https://www.nytimes.com/1992/03/23/us/minnesota-health-plan-accord.html"&gt;reporting&lt;/a&gt;, “About 30 states are studying legislation to provide health insurance to those who do not have it. But Minnesota has come closer to enacting a sweeping measure than any other state in the three years since Oregon created a plan to give coverage to all residents, in part by expanding eligibility for Medicaid.”&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Speaking about MinnesotaCare today, Carlson said he thought the investment could lead to healthier Minnesotans, and thereby a decreased strain on emergency medical services.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (662267) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;He also did not want any “important” legislation “to crash on the partisan rocks.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“I was independent of the GOP party leadership since I ran against the Republican Party’s endorsed candidate and was considered to be too liberal overall,” Carlson emailed while vacationing in Barcelona. “This environment meant that I was free to govern from the middle but lacked a strong party base in the Legislature. This led to building majority coalitions on every contentious issue. And it worked.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Murphy’s recollection is that Carlson and legislative Republicans finally went along with MinnesotaCare because it outlined how to pay for expanded health care. This included a 2% tax on the revenue of health care providers, a funding mechanism now used by most states.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“Minnesota was one of the first states in the nation to rely on a provider tax,” Murphy said.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;(MinnesotaCare’s other revenue stream? A tax hike on cigarettes for the princely sum of 5 cents a pack. It is now 79 cents per pack.)&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Childless adults were a population federal Medicaid would not touch until the 2010 Affordable Care Act.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;But in 1995, the Clinton administration granted Minnesota a federal waiver to fund parts of MinnesotaCare, including expanding the definition of a caretaker and streamlining benefits for pregnant women.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;MinnesotaCare ushered in an era of optimism. In 1997, the ad hoc Minnesota Health Care Commission &lt;a href="https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/pre2003/other/970182.pdf"&gt;released&lt;/a&gt; a survey finding that 90% of state residents were either “very satisfied” or “extremely satisfied” with their health care plan.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Kiscaden, who spoke on the phone from her living room overlooking a Mayo Clinic hospital, said she takes pride in MinnesotaCare.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“I was supportive because I represented a place that calls itself America’s city for health.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;h3 class="wp-block-heading"&gt;&lt;strong&gt;The Affordable Care Act fork in the road&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/h3&gt; &lt;p&gt;It was a mere decade and a half ago that the nation, or at least readers of &lt;a href="https://newrepublic.com/article/48298/check-out-tnrs-new-health-care-blog-the-treatment"&gt;political&lt;/a&gt; &lt;a href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/greenslade/2008/jun/25/whyjournalistsmustlearnthe"&gt;blogs&lt;/a&gt;, was totally obsessed with health care policy.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In 2010, President Barack Obama signed into law the Affordable Care Act, which created health insurance exchanges, outlawed insurers rejecting patients because of pre-existing conditions, and let children stay on their parents’ health care up until age 26.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198592) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;Almost lost in the shuffle was an ACA provision that expanded Medicaid eligibility to anyone at or below 138% of the federal poverty line. It was the first time adults without a child or a disability qualified for Medicaid. It was also the first time the federal government would foot the entire bill (today the feds fund 90% of the cost for this population segment).&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Once again, Minnesota was poised to leverage federal Medicaid money.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Except the &lt;a href="https://www.minnpost.com/eric-black-ink/2012/04/bachmann-meet-press-obama-health-care-dictator/"&gt;politics of Medicaid&lt;/a&gt; had changed. Not wanting to endorse anything associated with Obama, Republican Gov. Tim Pawlenty and the Republican-controlled Legislature rejected the ACA Medicaid expansion.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Abeler, a state representative at the time, said he and his fellow Republicans were worried that by insuring&nbsp;able-bodied adults, Minnesota was “moving away from the people who had genuine needs.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198602) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;During the 2010 legislative session, DFLers and Republicans struck a deal. If the Republican candidate for governor, Tom Emmer, won, they would stop their push for Medicaid expansion.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;But if Mark Dayton, the DFL candidate, won, he could sign an executive order accepting the Medicaid expansion.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Dayton won, leading to one of the liveliest signing ceremonies in Minnesota political history.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198589) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;“The new governor ended up with a standing-room-only audience of both protesters and supporters who crowded into the governor’s reception room, " MinnPost &lt;a href="http://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2011/01/daytons-medical-aid-signing-ceremony-turns-full-fledged-political-event/"&gt;reported&lt;/a&gt; at the time. “They clogged the doorway and even spilled into the hall.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“It was a gamble that paid off,” Murphy said of the 2010 deal. “There was no guarantee who was going to win.”&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;But the Republican-controlled legislature had second thoughts, balking at the executive order (and also killing any legislation to set up a health insurance exchange.)&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198591) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;With the state Legislature winding down its 2012 session, The New York Times &lt;a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2012/05/01/health/policy/in-fight-over-obama-health-law-a-front-in-minnesota.html"&gt;reported&lt;/a&gt;, “The federal divide over health care is reflected at the state level, even in Minnesota, which has a progressive tradition and a long history of innovation and bipartisan cooperation on health policy.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Once again, the Legislature punted until the election. And, once again, DFLers won, this time wresting control of the House and Senate in the November 2012 elections.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In February 2013, Dayton &lt;a href="https://www.mprnews.org/story/2013/02/19/dayton-signs-law-broadening-medicaid-in-mn"&gt;signed&lt;/a&gt; a bill legislatively codifying the Medicaid expansion.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198593) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;While all this was happening, DFLers were also trying to preserve MinnesotaCare.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;They lobbied Washington to keep this population on existing health plans, while shifting the cost to the federal government. They also wanted to keep MinnesotaCare around for those making 139% to 200% of the federal poverty level.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Tony Lourey, son of Becky Lourey and a DFL state senator from Kerrick at that time, recalls flights to Washington where he implored former Sen. Al Franken to give Minnesota another federal waiver.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“It was tricky getting the feds to grant our waiver,” Tony Lourey recalled. “They were pretty inundated with requests.”&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;On New Years Day 2015, a revised federal waiver &lt;a href="https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/section-1115-demonstrations/downloads/mn-pmap-pa.pdf"&gt;kicked in&lt;/a&gt;, giving subsidies to MinnesotaCare enrollees who earned too much to get on Medicaid.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The DFL political triumphs in the Dayton era had major consequences for Minnesotans and health care providers. Between 2013 and 2023, the number of state residents on Medical Assistance climbed more than 80% to nearly 1.4 million (it has decreased to 1.2 million since then).&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;h3 class="wp-block-heading"&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Medicaid under the microscope&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/h3&gt; &lt;p&gt;Medical Assistance has perennially been accompanied by audits showing that a hospital or treatment center or doctor’s office overbilled DHS or underbilled DHS, or that DHS got too much or too little money from the U.S. Department of Human Services.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Generally, this has been seen as the cost of operating a sprawling program serving a vulnerable population.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“It is just the nature of these programs,” Murphy said. “There are going to be people who choose to do the wrong thing.”&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (2198567) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;But the clamor over waste or even fraud in Medical Assistance has played at a higher volume in the Walz administration.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In 2019, the governor’s first year in office, an Office of the Legislative Auditor report found that Medical Assistance owed the federal government $48 million after it overpaid substance abuse treatment providers.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The audit coincided with Tony Lourey &lt;a href="https://www.minnpost.com/state-government/2019/07/resignation-puts-state-department-of-human-services-under-the-microscope-again/"&gt;resigning&lt;/a&gt; as DHS commissioner just six months into the job. A month after Lourey left, Deputy DHS Commissioner Claire Wilson departed as did Marie Zimmerman, an assistant commissioner who focused on Medical Assistance oversight.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;!-- RTT removed image (280585) --&gt; &lt;p&gt;Republicans pounced.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;"I think we have moved from a department in disarray to a department in distress," then-Sen. Scott Jensen, R-Chaska, told the Star Tribune at the time.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Walz named Jodi Harpstead the new DHS head, and she stayed on until this January. When Harpstead resigned, the Senate Republican caucus fired off a &lt;a href="https://www.mnsenaterepublicans.com/gruenhagen-dhs-commissioner-resigns-amid-fraud-scandals/"&gt;press release&lt;/a&gt; of bad-sounding news during her tenure with the headline, “DHS commissioner resigns amid fraud scandals.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;For instance, the release noted there was a December 2024 Office of Legislative Auditor &lt;a href="https://www.auditor.leg.state.mn.us/fad/2024/fad24-09.htm"&gt;report&lt;/a&gt; that discovered DHS “did not comply with significant-finance legal requirements we tested and generally had inadequate internal controls.”&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The audit found that DHS has not attempted to recover $40 million in overpayments to Medical Assistance providers.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;That’s a lot of wasted money. But the time period that the auditor looked at spanned between 2006 and 2023. In the year 2023 alone, the Medical Assistance budget &lt;a href="https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/forecastDHS_202411_tcm1053-658355.pdf"&gt;stood at&lt;/a&gt; $18.1 billion.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The Medical Assistance budget has been at least $10 billion every single year since 2015. DHS’s failure to recover a little over $2 million a year in provider overpayments is the equivalent of a millionaire forgetting to cash a $200 check.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Still, there &lt;a href="https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/05/05/fraud-proposals-focus-lawmakers-minnesota-legislative-session-ends?utm_campaign=Capitol_View_Newsletter20250505&amp;utm_medium=email&amp;utm_source=sfmc_&amp;utm_content="&gt;has been&lt;/a&gt; a bipartisan push to go after Medical Assistance fraud, with possible legislation including hiring more people to investigate provider claims.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Republicans are also pushing to remove undocumented immigrants from MinnesotaCare, though payments represent just a small part of the program.&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The real elephant in the room: costs for people who require continual, long-term care.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In his State of the State address this month, Walz alluded to these patients.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"&gt;&lt;img src="https://www.minnpost.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/MP-GovWalz-StofSt-640x432.jpg" alt="man gestures while speaking at podium" class="wp-image-2197350" /&gt;&lt;figcaption class="wp-element-caption"&gt;Gov. Tim Walz presents his State of the State address to a joint session of the legislature in the House Chamber the State Capitol, Wed., April 23, 2025.&lt;/figcaption&gt;&lt;/figure&gt; &lt;p&gt;“Right now, we’re the most generous state in the nation when it comes to these programs and services – and we should be proud of that,” Walz said.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Walz wants to cap this generosity a bit, noting that long-term care waivers will soon eat up one-eighth of the entire state budget. The DHS budget bill, which passed the House floor on Monday, is perhaps a start. It proposes $427 million in long-term care waiver &lt;a href="https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/OjSkGjW0UkKoWpn13mkGqQ.pdf"&gt;spending reductions&lt;/a&gt;.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;What neither the governor nor the Legislature have put forward is a contingency plan for significant federal Medicaid cuts.&nbsp;&lt;/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;“There is no way the state of Minnesota can come close to backfilling what is lost,” Walz said in April at the Martin Luther Campus, an assisted living facility in Bloomington, Minnesota, whose primary revenue source is Medicaid reimbursements. “We’re preparing for the worst-case scenario but it’s almost impossible.”&lt;/p&gt;
