Sign up MinnPost occasionally publishes letters to the editor. You can submit one at voices@minnpost.com. MinnesotaCare's origins President John F. Kennedy reminded us that “Success has many fathers, but failure is an orphan." How true. Just recently, two claimants came forth in MinnPost on May 7 and the Minnesota Star Tribune on May 10 to proclaim their role in developing the now heralded health care program, MinnesotaCare. As the governor who signed this legislation into law in 1992, I am familiar with its origin and development. During the 1991 legislative session, our administration was wrestling with an inherited deficit of $2.3 billion which approximated 10% of the budget. Then, the Legislature passed a major health care reform bill that significantly changed our system and cost over $500 million but did not provide any funding. I vetoed it but signaled that I was open to a more modest approach. I called in our state’s Commissioner of Health, Marlene Marschall, and outlined some guidelines for a possible approach. She and her deputy, Mary Jo O’Brien, quietly and effectively held endless meetings with leaders in the medical community and put together a proposal that was praised by Democrats and Republicans. Their work was the foundation of MinnesotaCare. From this point on, both parties worked together and the “Gang of Seven" was formed consisting of legislators from both houses and both parties and Curt Johnson, our director of policy. Frankly, their commitment and effort was outstanding and the final product was MinnesotaCare. However, the imposition of the 2% provider tax to finance it was a major stumbling block with many health care organizations causing some legislators, mainly Republican, to fall away. This placed considerable caucus pressure on DFL House Speaker Dee Long to let the proposal die since Republicans were faltering. She called me, explained the situation, and we agreed that this health care legislation was too important to crash on the rocks of partisanship. I twisted more arms but it was Speaker Long who got the bill passed. Finally, I salute the bipartisan cooperation of all four legislative leaders – Sen. Roger Moe (DFL Majority Leader), Sen. Duane Benson (GOP Minority Leader), Rep. Terry Dempsey, (GOP Minority Leader), and DFL House Speaker Dee Long. Now, at age 90, I appreciate more than ever the legislators who put the well-being of the public over all else and welcomed the expertise of agency leaders, professionals, and staff. This set the standard for positive governance. – Former Minnesota Gov. Arne Carlson Conservative voices to call out Trump Regarding the June 5 MinnPost commentary by the retired judge Mel Dickstein, who laments the lack of conservative voices to counter the lawlessness of the Trump administration: Indeed, there is a regrettable lack of conservatives, especially elected officials responding to the band of regressives, reactionaries, authoritarians and fascists who have hijacked the term “conservative.” But there is one very active website worthy of attention. It’s called The Bulwark , founded and mostly composed of former Republicans and other conservatives and moderates, many of whom worked for previous GOP administrations. They include Mona Charen, Bill Kristol, Tim Miller and a host of other voices who would be recognized as conservative. Pertinent to the judge’s interest is a weekly podcast featuring voter expert Sarah Longwell and attorney George Conway (ex- husband of Kelly Ann Conway, the former Trump aide). Sarah and George’s hourlong discussion probes details of the laws being broken by Trump and his fellow authoritarians along with the latest court action. Although The Bulwark welcomes paid subscribers, they offer lots of free content on their website and on YouTube. Their viewers and commenters range from conservative to moderate to liberals to progressive. Besides the focus on Trump, there is also room for discussion with pro-democracy Americans of all points of view. What’s considered important is that people with divergent views can disagree politely, then focus on the MAGA threat to the nation and the world. There may be other sources for people with the judge’s views. Perhaps if MinnPost resumed its Readers Comments section there could be enhanced communication between MinnPost members. Had it still existed it’s likely that the retired judge would not be a lone voice crying in the wilderness bemoaning the lack of fellow travelers. He could be a valuable contribution to the website that claims to value freedom of speech. – Roy Everson Wildfires endanger Minnesota’s peatlands – but we can protect them With embers from Minnesota’s wildfires still smoldering and Canadian smoke darkening our skies, it’s worth remembering that fires burn more than trees — they consume peatlands, a living foundation beneath our forests that has protected us for centuries. Once lost, these carbon-rich wetlands cannot be replaced. Peatlands are waterlogged wetlands filled with thousands of years of accumulated peat moss. Amazingly, they store 40% of the state’s terrestrial carbon, prevent floods and droughts, filter drinking water, and sustain vulnerable wildlife. But when they burn, their benefits vanish — and the carbon they release rivals the state’s largest polluters. I’m proud to call the Arrowhead region my home, and over the years, I’ve seen how peatlands anchor this truly special part of our state. Minnesota is second only to Alaska in peatland acreage, with over 6 million acres supporting rare orchids, butterflies, and songbirds. Globally, peatlands cover just 3% of Earth’s land but store 30% of its land-based carbon — double the carbon stored in all the world’s forests combined! Yet peatlands face growing threats, from historic drainage to modern disruptions like severe drought, mining, and logging. We must be smart about land management —protecting healthy peatlands and restoring degraded ones. Given their outsized importance, peatlands deserve greater prioritization. A few solutions: policies that help northern counties access state restoration funds, updating the Wetland Conservation Act rules to account for existing peatland carbon stores and not just their potential for future carbon storage, and designating ecosystem service benefits for peatlands on state managed land to maximize their long-term value. By acting now, Minnesota can safeguard its natural heritage while reducing catastrophic wildfire risks. Peatlands are our frontline defense—buffering floods, mitigating droughts, and shielding communities from deadly fires. If we delay, these critical ecosystems could vanish in smoke, taking their protections with them. – LeeAnn Geshick, Ely Energy efficiency empowers congregations like ours At Minneapolis Friends Meeting, our commitment to stewardship and sustainability is deeply rooted in our Quaker values. Recently, we installed solar panels on our meetinghouse, a project that aligns with our principles of environmental stewardship and promises to save us over $50,000 on energy bills over the system’s 25-year lifetime. This was made possible through federal energy tax credits available to nonprofits through Direct Pay. Traditionally, tax-exempt entities like ours couldn't benefit from energy tax deductions. Direct Pay allows nonprofits to receive the equivalent value of tax credits as direct payments, making upgrading our old energy systems with renewable energy technology financially feasible. Our transition to solar energy offers benefits that extend well beyond our meetinghouse. Our transition to solar energy contributes to Minnesota’s broader clean energy goals, supporting job creation, improving air quality, and fostering a more sustainable future. In our research and implementation process, we partnered with a range of organizations, including Minnesota Interfaith Power and Light, the Center for Energy and the Environment, RE-volv, and the City of Minneapolis Green Cost Share Program. We also received valuable guidance from solar energy consultant Ralph Jacobson and worked with energy contractors Cedar Creek Energy and Minify Energy to bring the project to life. Proposals to roll back provisions like Direct Pay and other energy tax credits threaten to dismantle the progress made for nonprofits. We were able to do this because of Direct Pay, and believe that communities across Minnesota should continue to have the benefits that come with an investment in this energy strategy. Our experience at Minneapolis Friends Meeting demonstrates the tangible benefits of these incentives. These tools are essential for enabling nonprofits, schools, churches, and local governments to reduce energy costs, and they must be protected to foster a more sustainable and affordable future for all. We have learned a tremendous amount about energy, government workings, and can be a resource to others through our own experience. – Rick VandenDolder and Roger Miller Want to add your voice?
