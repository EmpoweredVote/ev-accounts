Credit: REUTERS/Shannon Stapleton D.C. Memo | A recap of a week’s worth of Washington political news, journalism, and opinion, delivered with an eye toward what matters for Minnesota. Sign up WASHINGTON – At first, Pete Hegseth’s most glaring problem as Donald Trump’s candidate to run the Pentagon was his inexperience. Now it’s his baggage. In the days after Trump selected Hegseth to be his secretary of defense, the nominee quickly came under scrutiny for misogynistic statements about women in combat, a tattoo on his biceps bearing a slogan favored by Christians who fought Muslims in the Crusades (and more recently by white supremacists), a payment to a woman who accused him of sexual assault, and more. Rep. Betty McCollum, D-4 th District, the senior Democrat on a U.S. House appropriations panel with jurisdiction over the Pentagon’s $825 billion budget, took issue with Hegseth’s comments that women in the military are a distraction to male troops. “They said things like that in my grandmother’s day,” McCollum said. Hegseth has suggested he wants to eliminate “woke” military officers and has written contemptuously of laws and treaties that constrain soldiers, called the Geneva Conventions outdated and persuaded Trump to pardon U.S. service members who had been accused of war crimes. Democrats on the Senate Armed Services Committee, the panel that would vet Hegseth’s nomination, have expressed skepticism of Hegseth’s thin resume compared with those who’ve held the job before. Even a Republican on the committee has indicated concern over the allegation of a sexual assault during a 2017 conference of Republican women in California. “I think he's a good pick, but once again, as allegations come out, we'll figure out … if he can get confirmed or not,” Sen. Markwayne Mullin of Oklahoma said on Sunday’s Meet the Press. Yet Hegseth’s lack of experience and troublesome history may not keep him from confirmation to the job of running the nation’s military forces as Trump has indicated he may push through at least some of his nominees in an unconventional and unprecedented manner that would bypass the Senate’s confirmation process. Loyalty trumps experience Hegseth, 44, was born in Minneapolis and graduated near the top of his class at Forest Lake Area High School. But he appears to have had his political awakening at Princeton University, where he wrote for the conservative student magazine called The Princeton Tory, advocating for “traditional family values” and a “return of the acceptability of the ‘homemaker’ vocation.” Hegseth was critical of the campus chapter of the Organization of Women Leaders (OWL) and as publisher of the Tory ran a cover story that featured an illustration of an owl in a gun’s crosshairs. He joined the Army National Guard and served overseas in Afghanistan and Iraq as well as at Guantanamo Bay. Hegseth also tried his hand at politics in 2012, seeking the GOP nomination to run against Democratic Sen. Amy Klobuchar but dropping out of the race after the state Republican convention. He bolstered his conservative credentials as the former head of Concerned Veterans for America, a group backed by billionaires Charles and David Koch. But he is known to most Americans as a weekend host of the talk show “Fox and Friends,” a Trump favorite, where he bonded with the president-elect over shared views about the military. “Donald Trump campaigned on an America First policy that is skeptical of U.S. military intervention overseas and Hegseth shares those views,” said Columbus State University military history professor David Kieran. Kieran, who is also the Colonel Richard R. Hallock Distinguished University Chair in Military History, said that in his first term, Trump “did have a tense relationship” with some of Pentagon leaders. “(Trump) seems now to be choosing people whom he thinks will be loyal to him and carry out his policies,” Kieran said. He also said Hegseth is a “non-traditional choice” to head the Defense Department and its 3 million employees. Traditionally, the top civilian job at the Pentagon is offered to someone in the ranks of senior military leadership, like current Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin, or from positions of authority in military or foreign affairs matters, like former Sen. Chuck Hagel, Kienan said. He also said that there have been occasional nominees that come from private industry where they have been proven to be good managers of large, complex organizations, such as former Defense Secretary Robert McNamara, who was the first president of Ford Motor Company to come from outside the Ford family. Defense analyst John Arquilla, however, said Hegseth, a combat veteran with knowledge of the military, may not be such a poor choice, especially since it may be time to “shake up” the Pentagon and its traditional ways of doing things. “(Hegseth) has served with considerable distinction and is well in line with President Trump’s broader ideas about the future direction of the military,” said Arquilla, author of an upcoming book called “The Troubled American Way of War.” One new role of the U.S. military may be the implantation of the new Trump administration’s immigration policies. On Monday, Trump posted “TRUE!!!” on social media in response to a conservative commentator who wrote that the new president would declare a national emergency and use military assets to lead “a mass deportation program.” Short-circuiting the Senate process Trump bolted out of the gate with a number of controversial cabinet choices that have raised eyebrows in the U.S. Senate, including former Rep. Matt Gaetz as attorney general, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. as secretary of Health and Human Services and former Hawaii Rep. Tulsi Gabbard as director of national intelligence. With Hegseth and those other picks, Trump prized loyalty over experience and they may all face a tough time during their Senate confirmation hearings. “Trump is not at all unwilling to risk public hearings for his chosen cabinet nominees,” Arquilla said. But Trump has also indicated he’s willing to use a process outside the Senate’s traditional role of vetting candidates, and that’s by taking advantage of a recess of the House and Senate to make his appointments. Normally, a nominee is considered by the Senate committee with jurisdiction over the agency in which the candidate would serve. So Gaetz would be subject to a hearing by the Senate Judiciary Committee and Hegseth would be questioned by the Senate Armed Services Committee. Nominees would also be subjected to FBI background checks. But Trump is distrustful of the agency, saying it has unfairly targeted him for political reasons, and has indicated he wants private companies to conduct these background checks. Nominees are also required to fill out voluminous questionnaires by the Senate committees that are vetting them, as well as disclose their finances. Confirmation hearings on Trump nominees would begin shortly before his Jan. 20 inauguration, or shortly thereafter. But Trump could also short circuit the process with a recess appointment, which would put his candidates in their jobs until the end of the 119 th Congress, or for about two years. Even if incoming Senate Majority Leader John Thune, R-South Dakota, keeps the Senate in session to avoid a recess appointment, a provision in the U.S. Constitution – which has never been used before – could allow the House to adopt a concurrent resolution that provided for the adjournment of both the U.S. House and the Senate. If the Senate didn’t adopt the resolution, the Constitution gives Trump the authority to adjourn both chambers for at least 10 days, and perhaps longer. Trump could then use the resulting intrasession recess to appoint his nominees. Trump, of course, could also withdraw nominees who might not win enough GOP support in the Senate, where Republicans will hold a 53-47 majority in the next Congress. Democrats would likely be united in rejecting controversial nominees. But as of Tuesday afternoon, Trump continued to say he supported all of his controversial nominees, even making phone calls to key senators on behalf of Gaetz, who was under investigation by the House Ethics Committee before he resigned last week. The panel was looking into allegations that Gaetz “engaged in sexual misconduct and illicit drug use, accepted improper gifts, dispensed special privileges and favors to individuals with whom he had a personal relationship and sought to obstruct government investigations of his conduct.” Gaetz has denied wrongdoing. But several Republican senators said they would like to see a copy of the House Ethics Committee’s report, which was in draft form before Gaetz resigned from Congress, ending the panel’s probe into his conduct.
