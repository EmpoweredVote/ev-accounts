Sign up Julie Leon, age 65, died in Seattle on June 28, 2021. The cause of death? Hyperthermia, a body temperature of 110 degrees. She had literally baked to death in Seattle’s ‘heat dome,’ a 27-day heat wave across Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia. The day that Ms. Leon died, the air had reached 108 degrees. She had gone to a doctor’s appointment that morning. As she was driving home, the heat rose rapidly, and her car had no air conditioning. She pulled off the highway onto a residential street, and by then she was too debilitated to call for help. Two hours later, a passerby found her, unconscious. An estimated 1,400 people died during that heat dome. Experts say the heat dome could not have happened without human-driven climate change. On May 29, 2025, Ms. Leon’s daughter Misti, on behalf of her mother’s estate, filed a wrongful-death lawsuit in Seattle’s King County Superior Court against ExxonMobil, BP, Chevron, Shell, and other giant fossil-fuel companies for her mother’s death. She accuses the companies of “a campaign of deception” to minimize the risks of using fossil fuels, asserting that the deadly consequences have been well known since the 1950s, when her mother was born. The Union of Concerned Scientists affirms that instead of acknowledging the harmful effects of their products and committing to swift and deep reductions in polluting emissions, the world’s largest fossil fuel companies have knowingly deceived the public — and they continue to do so today. The fossil fuel industry spent $445 million to influence the 2024 elections, including direct donations, lobbying, and advertising. That’s only in this past election. The industry sowed doubt about climate science for decades, spending $2.9 billion on false-claim advertising in a 10-year period and $1.3 billion more lobbying to shape public policy on energy issues during the same period. It has pumped out $827.9 million in campaign contributions since 2000 to elect sympathetic officials at local, state, and federal levels and more than $5 billion undermining climate science and fighting clean energy policies. We are in an “Oiligarchy.” In 2024 alone, the U.S. experienced 27 billion-dollar weather and climate disasters, with a total cost of $182.7 billion. These events included severe storms, tropical cyclones, droughts, and wildfires. Adding the 2024 events to the record since 1980, the U.S. has experienced 403 such disasters, with total costs exceeding $2.915 trillion. These events included 32 droughts, 45 floods, nine freeze events, 203 severe storms, 67 cyclones, 23 wildfires, and 24 winter storms. The 1980-2024 annual average is nine events; the annual average for the most recent 5 years (2020–2024) is 23 events. But Mrs. Leon’s daughter Misti is part of a grassroots and global effort pushing back against ecocide. The term ‘ecocide’ was first used in 1970 by Professor Arthur Galston, a biologist at Yale University. He used it to describe the devastation that the U.S. caused by spraying 20 million gallons of the chemical Agent Orange in Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos during the Vietnam War. Galston advocated for an international agreement to ban ecocide, the “devastation and destruction which aim at damaging or destroying the ecology of geographic areas to the detriment of all life, whether human, animal, or plant.” Agent Orange defoliated the jungle and caused cancers, neurological disease, and birth defects in at least 3 million people. The legal definition of ecocide is “unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge that there is a substantial likelihood of severe and either widespread or long-term damage to the environment being caused by those acts.” In today’s absence of federal action to halt the progression of the climate crisis, city and state actors must step up to address ecocide. In 2007, Minnesota passed the Next Generation Climate Act, requiring the state to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 80% between 2005 and 2050 while maintaining reliable and affordable energy. At the time, Minnesota was ranked the number two state — second to California — for passing aggressive reductions in greenhouse gas emissions. In 2023, the state Legislature updated these goals to reduce greenhouse gas emissions 50% by 2030 from a 2005 baseline and achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. Twenty-four states plus the District of Columbia have adopted specific greenhouse gas emission reduction targets. The 2025 Minnesota Senate, in action led by state Sen. Sandy Pappas, designated April as Ecocide Awareness and Prevention Month. Ellen J. Kennedy At a global level, the European Parliament voted unanimously in 2023 to criminalize ecocide, as have several dozen countries around the world. At the International Criminal Court, on Dec. 24, 2024, a proposal was introduced to criminalize ecocide with the same consequence as the existing human rights crimes of genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes, and to end impunity for the leading perpetrators by holding them financially and legally responsible for their global devastation. Despite the White House desire to ‘drill, baby, drill,’ and to roll back every effort to prevent and mitigate deadly fossil fuel pollution and damage, we are seeing local and global actors stepping up – because we know that there is no Planet B. World Without Genocide will hold a webinar, Genocides and Environmental Destruction: From the Holocaust to Today, on Thursday, June 26, from 7 p.m.-8:30 p.m. CT. The event is open to the public; continuing education credits are available for Minnesota lawyers, teachers, nurses, and social workers. Register at www.worldwithoutgenocide.org/destruction Ellen J. Kennedy, Ph.D., is founder and executive director of World Without Genocide. Want to add your voice?
