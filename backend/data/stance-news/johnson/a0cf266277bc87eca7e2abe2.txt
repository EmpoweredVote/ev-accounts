Credit: Supplied Paulette Johnson described her brother John’s move from one nursing home to another as “traumatic.” John Johnson had lived in the Meadow Lane Restorative Care Center in Benson for about five years before he had to leave when the center announced its closure last summer. Since the COVID pandemic, nursing homes across Minnesota have been closing at alarming rates, due to financial stressors. From 2019 to 2022, 15 nursing homes shut down in Minnesota. In 2023, eight nursing homes closed. The closures not only affect residents; they also have an impact on families, communities and the nursing home employees. While he was at the Meadow Lane home, Johnson was very active in Benson, his hometown. He’d go into town with his scooter, attend church, go to football games and also run the scoreboard for softball and baseball games. John Johnson running the scoreboard at a high school baseball game in Benson, back when he lived in the Meadow Lane Restorative Care Center. Credit: Courtesy of Paulette Johnson “He's extremely attached to the community. We cried probably for about a week,” Paulette said of his move to another home. “It was very traumatic.” It’s hard for her, too, since she lives in Benson and he is now in a nursing home in Litchfield, about an hour’s drive to the east. “Johnny and I are very close. I was up at the (Benson) nursing home probably two, three, four times a week, bringing him this and that,” she said. Johnson’s specific needs require him to have a nurse at all times, which limited his care options in Benson. So he and his family decided the best home for him would be the one in Litchfield. Now she says she sees him around holidays, birthdays and county fairs. She thinks that come fall, she might see him only once a month. Strengthened regulations Benson resident Grant Herfindahl had a similar experience after his mom’s nursing home in Glenwood closed in 2014 and he was left in the frenzy of finding a new home. Herfindahl regularly attends meetings of the Minnesota Farmers Union and brought up the nursing home closure in Benson at a recent gathering. He hopes there can be more legislation regarding letting communities know and provide input on nursing home closures. Stu Lourey, the government relations director for the farmers union, said this has been a concern from other members across the state, too. Sign up Current state statute requires hospitals to provide a 120-day notice before closures and for the commissioner of public health to host a public hearing regarding the closure. Nursing homes don’t operate under the same state guidelines, though they do have some , like a requirement that they provide a 60-day notice before closures or a change in operations and assist residents in finding new homes. Herfindahl thinks regulations for nursing homes should be similar to those of hospitals as more nursing homes continue to close in Greater Minnesota. In 2023, Minnesota committed $300 million to help struggling nursing homes over the next two years. Despite some of that funding being dispersed last August, three nursing homes still closed that year. A nursing home in Red Wing is currently under the process of closing and another in Arlington is set to close by the end of August. Herfindahl recalled what it was like when his mom’s home closed. He remembers being notified around 60 days before. “The burden was completely on me calling these different nursing homes, and trying to find a little bit about them,” he said. His mom ended up in a nursing home 30 miles away, doubled up in a room with somebody else. He said her situation there was much more depressing than the one in Glenwood and thinks her condition worsened after she moved. “I wouldn't have wanted to be in my mother's place, because you’ve just gotten kind of comfortable after a few years in the nursing home with friends and staff,” he said. Herfindahl knew Johnson because of Johnson’s presence within the community. He said a nursing home closure affects things beyond the individual who moves. “Then there's someone that becomes less of a cheerleader for the community and has to adjust to being in a totally different environment,” Herfindahl said. Sustained closures From 2019 to 2022, 15 nursing homes shut down in Minnesota. In 2023, eight nursing homes closed. According to a 2023 survey by Long-Term Care Imperative, a collaboration of aging services providers, 15% of nursing homes statewide had exhausted their financial reserves and 10% were considering closing. In Greater Minnesota, 17% had no financial reserves and 12% considered closures, according to the survey. With nursing homes becoming fewer and farther between in rural areas, people are less able to fit everything into their schedules as drives to visit loved ones become longer. “For the average person running and checking on mom, even for people that work, or have small children or children — you can't just drop by mom, because it's a two-hour process to drive up and visit with mom for an hour,” he said. “That's really kind of one of the downsides of communities losing their nursing homes.” Two rounds of state fund ing Nursing homes were in a tough financial situation in 2020, having lost employees and residents and because of changing Medicaid reimbursement rates. What state Sen. John Hoffman, DFL-Champlin, referred to as the “great resignation” combined with high inflation during COVID created a perfect storm in which nursing homes didn’t receive sufficient reimbursements. The $300 million was part of an infrastructure bonding deal struck at the Legislature in 2023. Nursing homes received one-time payments of $225,000 while the remaining funds were determined by a formula based on the number of active nursing facility beds, separated in two payments — one in August 2023 and one in August 2024 and totaling $173.5 million. The second round of funds from that legislation is now making its way to nursing homes across the state in cities like Rochester, suburbs such as Fridley and in rural towns like St. Peter, Ely and International Falls. The remaining funds were put into a Workforce Incentive Fund that facilities can use for hiring and retention bonuses, employee-owned benefits and employee 401(k) contributions, along with professional development, childcare, meals, transportation and housing needs of employees. That fund caps out at $3,000 per worker each year and is available until funds are spent or through July 2029. Hoffman said the funding will allow nursing homes to have cash on hand to hire people and receive sufficient reimbursement. To some, like Tom Opatz, one of the founders of Cura, a nonprofit with 12 care centers in rural Minnesota that received some funding from the pool, these funds are not a long-term solution. “It's a band-aid to a long problem of paying for workers to work in health care,” he said. Staff retention has remained a challenge, but average wages of $22-25 per hour for Certified Nursing Assistants has been critical for maximizing their reimbursement, he said. And with that pay, it’s easier to create incentives for a job that many people would not be interested in. “Unlike other professions where you can work from home when you're providing care for seniors, you actually have to go to work,” he said. “You physically have to go there. It's humans taking care of humans.” Cura has also tried to stay afloat by putting its nursing homes in areas with viable hospitals, like Paynesville, Long Prairie and Melrose, for example, where there will likely be high demand as people who are hospitalized are discharged. But in areas with less population density, and even for his own organization, he thinks there needs to be more funding directed towards elderly care. “I think as a society, there's going to be choices on where we're going to invest our money because there is only so much money,” Opatz said. “Nursing homes are important to me, because that's my profession. (For) teachers, schools are important to them. The doctors and nurses, hospitals are important to them.” Ava Kian Ava Kian is MinnPost's Greater Minnesota reporter.
