Credit: MinnPost photo by Winter Keefer St. Paul's rent control debate is back this week as the City Council considers further loosening the controversial policy. Community members will have a chance to weigh in on both rent control and tenant protection ordinances during the Wednesday council meeting. Here’s all you need to know leading up to the 3:30 p.m. hearing at City Hall: What the council is considering Council members have brought forward two ordinances meant to serve in tandem: one amending the city’s rent stabilization legislative code and another creating a chapter pertaining to tenant protections. The rent stabilization amendment is sponsored by council members Anika Bowie, Saura Jost and Rebecca Noecker, and the tenant protection measure is sponsored by Cheniqua Johnson, Hwa Jeong Kim, Noecker and Jost. The once-sweeping rent control cap that limits increases to no more than 3% in a 12-month period passed via a 2021 city referendum already has been whittled down with the addition of a 20-year exemption for new development. This change was proposed after developers said rent control was making new projects unaffordable. Overall, residential development declined across the county over the past few years, in part because interest rates rose. However, some high-profile St. Paul projects have been left on pause with developers blaming rent control for trouble securing financing. That list includes Ryan Co.’s housing development at Highland Bridge . Daily Newsletter | MinnPost's top stories delivered to your inbox every weekday. Sign up Related: Cityscape: Housing construction in Minneapolis and St. Paul is tanking The new amendment echoes a proposal made last year by Mayor Melvin Carter that would make properties built after Dec. 31, 2004, indefinitely exempt from rent control. Noecker, the council’s president, has emphasized that this proposed amendment is purposely being presented side-by-side with the proposed tenant protection ordinance. That measure would provide renters with protection against discrimination from landlords, require property owners to provide renters “fair and sufficient” notice of the sale of their home in the case of possible eviction, and limit the amount a landlord can charge for a security deposit. This is not the first attempt to write tenant protections into the city’s legislative code. A sweeping tenant protection ordinance passed in 2021 also addressed those circumstances but ultimately was blocked by a federal judge after landlords filed a lawsuit. Noecker said the new ordinance includes weaker language around “just cause” to avoid a similar legal ruling. How rent control plays into other St. Paul issues Rent control is back on the table amid a range of key issues facing St. Paul — most recently decisions impacting trash collection and the council’s failure to appoint an interim member to fill former council President Mitra Jalali’s seat. These issues are not wholly unrelated. The recent trash debacle is over a property bought by FCC Environmental, the city’s new waste pickup provider. Carter called a state of emergency last week after the council took action blocking FCC Environmental’s use and development of land at 540 Randolph Ave. The council ultimately extended the mayor’s state of emergency for 90 days. While the mayor would like the council to reverse its decision (which really comes down to zoning) council members have said this time should instead be used to find an alternative site for FCC Environmental. The owner of the Randolph Avenue property said in January that he had been planning to build housing on the site — the preferred alternative for neighbors and council members — but the city’s rent control policy had made it “no longer economically viable.” Related: Cityscape: Three things to know about St. Paul’s latest development spat, and why neighborhood organizers are right this time Housing issues also are linked to the failed council appointment for the council’s Ward 4 seat. Last week, the council failed to reach consensus over who should fill the interim seat before its April 7 deadline. This left the decision up to Carter, who on Monday announced his appointment of Jalali’s former aide Matt Privratsky to the seat. During an impromptu recess called after the split, council member Johnson pulled reporters into her office to object to the way council members were conducting business. The council’s votes were split while considering two candidates: Privratsky and Union Park District Council member Lisa Nelson. Johnson alleged she was approached with a quid pro quo proposition on the interim appointment. Specifically, she said a member of the council reached out to her (she did not specify who) and said that if she voted for any candidate other than Privratsky, the unnamed council member would support the rent control and renters' rights ordinances. “I am incredibly concerned about the fact that we think it’s OK to exchange support for candidates for policy issues that committee members have been waiting for years for, and I genuinely don’t want to ever be in a decision where I’m having to choose between an interim council member and tenant protections,” Johnson said last week. “I don’t want that to be the discussion, and I’m not willing to have that be the arrangement here.” Support the news you rely on You rely on MinnPost to report on the things that matter to you.
