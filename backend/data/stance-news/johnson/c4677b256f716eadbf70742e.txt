Sign up Ever since the pandemic, downtown Minneapolis has been plagued with empty storefronts and vacancy rates in office buildings that would have been seen as unacceptable just a decade prior. Considering these troubling trends, the city, as well as other private interest groups with a stake in downtown, have indicated the need for investment and improvement. The city’s response has consisted primarily of convening task forces and work groups, such as Mayor Jacob Frey’s Vibrant Storefronts Work Groups , to endlessly brainstorm ideas on how to resuscitate downtown. Plans have included support for converting commercial space to residential space, examples of which include the recently converted Northstar Center or the closing off Nicollet Mall to traffic. Non-governmental entities, such as the Minneapolis Foundation , have released extensive reports detailing a plan for revitalizing downtown. One thing both governmental and non-governmental groups share is the structure by which they have developed their plans and visions for downtown, and that is from the top. Centralized approaches to decision making have become the norm in many American cities over the past decade, with community outreach existing as more or less of a formality in many cases. While citizens are encouraged to provide feedback on city initiatives, rarely are they the ones given the tools and resources to come up with, plan and execute ideas and visions for their communities. Parallels can be drawn between the current state of downtown and the state of many of the city’s non-downtown neighborhoods leading into the 1990s and the conception of the Neighborhood Revitalization Program . Suburban flight was wreaking havoc on inner city neighborhoods throughout the 1970s and 80s; as more affluent individuals and families left, the result was a dramatically shrunken tax base. Faced with a potentially generation-defining issue, the city was forced to act. By delegating authority over decision making to neighborhood associations themselves, they hoped that investments would be more localized, efficient and directly beneficial to citizens. This revolutionary idea soon materialized in 1990 as the Neighborhood Revitalization Program. The program had the lofty goal of distributing $400 million in funding over the course of 20 years, nothing short of a windfall for many small neighborhood associations. The NRP drastically altered the landscape of neighborhood associations in Minneapolis. What were once underfunded, small groups that relied primarily on volunteer labor transformed almost overnight into organizations that had the financial resources to enact dramatic change in their neighborhoods. Overall, neighborhoods used just under half of their allocated NRP dollars to construct housing projects — in many cases affordable housing projects. The third largest allocation of funds went towards economic development and efforts that aimed to revitalize commercial corridors and help support local businesses. The results show that neighborhoods channeled funding in a way that mirrored neighborhood needs, meaning investment was sensitive to the local context. The bottom-up design of the NRP led to increases in housing stock, revitalization of many key commercial corridors, improvement of public infrastructure such as parks and schools, and increases in the institutional capacity of many neighborhood associations. While the NRP ended in the mid-2000s, its legacy, and the outcomes of its approach to citizen participation and planning, can help inform future approaches to neighborhood level revitalization. Alexander Kurt Johnson Downtown is currently suffering from many of the same symptoms that were remedied through the NRP — primarily a shortage of affordable housing, vacant storefronts and a lack of investment in the public realm. The NRP provides an imperfect, but useful, lens by which to approach the behemoth that is the decay of downtown. Of course, steps should be taken to shore up weaknesses that were exposed throughout the course for the NRP. Future decentralized approaches must do more to ensure that neighborhood organizations are representative of all residents, and that funding is allocated in an equitable and progressive way that benefits neighbors of all backgrounds and incomes. In an age of atrophying social capital, and increasing distrust of the government, the city needs to place more faith in its citizens as local experts of their neighborhoods and help cultivate a civic space that encourages and supports bottom-up decision making. Afterall, the format of the NRP produced results. Who’s to say a similar approach, whatever it may look like, won’t help breathe life back into downtown?
