Dear Minnesotans, When Robert Redford died last September, I rewatched “All the President’s Men,” which, for those not as into 1970s movies as I am, is the true story of Bob Woodward (played by Redford) and Carl Bernstein (played by Dustin Hoffman) as Washington Post reporters breaking the Watergate scandal. What is most striking about this 50-year-old movie was how many doors Woodward and Bernstein knocked on. They knocked on doors of low-level employees for the Committee to Reelect the President (CREEP), officials in Richard Nixon’s Justice Department and, of course, their editor Ben Bradlee. Even though they met with people who could have their livelihoods ruined or be sent to prison by talking about Watergate, people routinely let Woodward and Bernstein in . They often even made the reporters a pot of coffee. Now, this was a dramatic reenactment! But it evoked a pre-smart phone (pre-Walkman, even!) era when talking IRL to strangers was more accepted, even entering into their private homes. Daily Newsletter | MinnPost's top stories delivered to your inbox every weekday. Sign up One of the few realms where door-knocking remains is political campaigns. Candidates I have interviewed treat the practice as a sacred and irreplaceable ritual. Sen. Jim Abeler, R-Anoka, keeps a tally on how many neighbors he has greeted. Abeler’s likely general election opponent, Angela Nelson, posts videos of her door-knocking duties But how many people answer? “Everybody has Ring cameras now,” said Cole Birkeland, an attorney in Lake Elmo and the DFL-endorsed candidate for Minnesota House District 41A. “You can see the camera light up when it starts filming.” Birkeland recalled door-knocking with his wife and dealing with five consecutive unanswered knocks or rings. But on the sixth try, someone was hosting a watch party for the Masters golf tournament. Birkeland was invited in and found himself striking up conversations with several registered voters. Incumbent 41A Rep. Wayne Johnson, R-Cottage Grove, is pacing himself, but says he will door knock more after Labor Day. “I haven’t hit the doors myself nearly as much,” he said. “I feel like a lot of people are either concentrating on the (Aug. 11) primaries or are on vacation.” Jen Fox, the DFL-endorsed candidate in 41B, said door-knocking can make her scared. “Sometimes I have to take like five deep breaths because it is very intimidating,” Fox said. Fox encounters people who get annoyed, but also constituents champing at the bit to chat. “A lot of people are eager to be heard,” she said. One problem with door-knocking is that some doors, and the voters behind those doors, can’t be accessed. I live in an apartment with no ringer. Politicians can only visit me through texting or teleporting. Meanwhile, in rural areas voters could live too far apart to be worth visiting. The stereotypical image of door-knocking is soliciting a single-family home. Would you open your door to a political candidate? Email me at mblake@minnpost.com . Support Capitol Conversations!
