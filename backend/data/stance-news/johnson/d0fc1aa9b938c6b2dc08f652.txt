Sign up Tick. Tick. Tick. I’m seated at the counsel’s table at the federal dourthouse in Minneapolis watching the clock. Every second. Every tick. Every tock. It is nine on the dot. Tick. Tick. Tick — “All rise!” My heart jumps out of my chest. And I jump out of my seat. I gained a lot of respect for our justice system watching that clock tick. The courthouse felt solemn and hallowed for a young lawyer. It is what I read about in books, watched on television. Days are gone when I’m staying in a motel in Schaumburg, Illinois, or driving from New York to Madison, New Jersey. I doubt there are many lawyers willing to travel to these little known parts of the country. I suppose there are even fewer clients willing to foot the bill. Why spend all of that money on airfare, hotel, car rental, meals — not to mention all the time — when you can just hop on Zoom ? I’ll probably never visit Galveston, Texas, again. At least, after depositions ended early I saw the Johnson Space Center — a half hour’s drive away. I experienced the world the best way possible: in three-dimensions. I can’t imagine people would take much interest in a tale about a lawyer arguing with his screen. It’d be as romantic as a dating app love affair, as dramatic as an argument over text. Related: As attendance declines amid federal enforcement actions, St. Paul schools offer virtual option Today, my world is divided. Half the time I’m in-person at the courthouse. The other half I might as well be in my own television show, with grainy video, muffled sound and all of the associated technical difficulties. At the end of a long day in Hennepin County, I’ll look a client in the eyes and shake her hand. If I’m on Zoom, I’ll settle for an emoji. No doubt Zoom is convenient . No traffic, no looking for a parking spot, no calculating milage, no storing receipts. You can save a few minutes, or a couple hours. Time is money. And lost time is never found again. But there are things that I miss about the courtroom — literally. For example, I might not see opposing counsel today. “Your honor,” he says, “my camera doesn’t work.” “That’s all right, counselor. Go on with your argument.” I might, for a brief while, not even see my client. “Call your first witness.” “Your honor, I call—" “Wait! Your witness isn’t here. Now that I look at it, your client isn’t here either!” How could I lose my client? Mr. Johnson was not permitted to enter his own trial. He didn’t have the password. And who is that “observer” behind the blank screen? I’ll never know. I fumble through my desktop, filled with various pleadings, exhibits, orders. Too many applications running, too many icons and windows open. Somehow I can’t find the right window. Where is everyone? Here they are, but now no one can see my exhibits. I don’t have permission to share my screen. In less than a minute I can be in three different rooms. Sometimes people just disappear. Other times I disappear. Forget suits and ties. I’ll find a lawyer with an open collar, reclining back in his seat. My client is in a different room, just a click away. Click. “Who are you?” he asks. “Well, I’m your lawyer,” I say, taking a sip of coffee. “Oh, I’ve been waiting for you,” he says, leaning back on his couch, taking a long puff from his vape. “I apologize for the wait, but may I say, you do look pretty comfortable.” He exhales the smoke. “Anyway,” I go on, “I’ve had a chance to review your file, and my advice is&#8230;" The screen momentarily goes blank, and like Spock I’m instantly transported to a new reality. “The judge wants to see you,” the clerk tells me. My brain has to quickly readjust. I’m inside the courtroom now. Related: Returning remote workers add some bustle to downtown Minneapolis I see the virtual background of the court, the judge fading in and out. Other times there’s no need for pretense, no background at all. I’m being questioned by a judge in her kitchen—it looks nice. What’s for lunch? Don’t get me wrong. It’s still serious, still consequential. But I didn’t become a lawyer to sit behind the computer and argue with my screen. That’s not what Atticus Finch did — and, one time, we all wanted to be Atticus Finch. As I await a cold burger from Uber Eats, I wonder about how streamlined life has become, though I can’t shake the feeling that something is missing. The virtual courtroom just doesn’t have that smell, that sweet, woody smell of archaic legal records and law books, aging mahogany and varnish. It makes me reminisce about the Ramsey County courthouse. Now that’s a courthouse! Its rooms should be filled with suits, and arguments, and the sounds of pounding gavels. You’d need to invest in a pair of good headphones to truly hear the thud of a gavel on Zoom. And you can never look a witness directly in the eyes. Nor can you be sure what they ’re looking at. “Sir, are you reading from your notes?” “Um, yes.” “Please don’t do that.” I long for the feeling of sitting in a stiff chair, watching the clock tick, before the chambers door opens. Those chairs aren’t comfortable. They’re not supposed to be. You shouldn’t get too relaxed when there’s so much at stake. You feel the anticipation. Something profound is about to happen. Then you rise. Out of respect not just for the judge but for the rule of law. Opposing counsel rises for their argument. Then you rise again for yours. If we all rose now…? Well, you know what they say about the emperor — maybe it’s true of someone in the courtroom. Perhaps, we should all invest in a standing desk. Vadim Trifel is a Project Housing Attorney with the Volunteer Lawyers Network (VLN). He regularly represents low-income tenants in Hennepin County. VLN is always looking for lawyers to staff both their in-person and Zoom clinics. Want to add your voice?
