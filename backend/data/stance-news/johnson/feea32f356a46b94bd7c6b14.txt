Sign up It’s a good time to consider the mix of pets and presidential campaigns in light of Donald Trump and JD Vance’s prevarications about Haitian immigrants eating stolen dogs and cats. It’s a fact that presidents and pets have long been part of American life. George Washington owned over 50 dogs and bred the first American foxhound. Lincoln was very fond of cats and had two — Tabby and Dixie — while in the White House. While Theodore Roosevelt was president he had a pig, a badger, a lizard, a blue macaw, five guinea pigs and a small bear named Jonathan Edwards. Likewise, pets have been a bright and disarming spot in most presidential campaigns. The first campaign pet story I know of is from the 1856 presidential contest when Republican John Fremont faced off against Democrat James Buchanan. According to the told-from-the-stump tale, Ohio Republicans were having a meeting and a boy was there selling four newly-born pups. An attendee asked, “Are these pups for Fremont?” The boy replied, “Yes sir,” so the man purchased two of them. A week later, Democrats met at the same place in Ohio and the boy was trying to sell his two remaining pups. He was asked what kind of pups they were and replied, “These are Democratic pups.” The boy was overheard by the Republican who had bought the first two pups, who angrily asked, “Last week didn’t you say the pups I bought were Republican?” “Yes sir,” the boy replied, “but these ain’t, they’ve got their eyes open.” Proving there is little new in campaigns, in 1952 Republicans told the same story but claimed this time that the pups with their eyes open were for the GOP’s candidate, Dwight Eisenhower. Politicians know voters will usually side with animals. In 1908, President Theodore Roosevelt cautioned his chosen successor and GOP candidate, 300-pound William Howard Taft, against riding horses because it would be “dangerous to you and cruelty to the horse.” After being photographed lifting his beagle by the ears in the early stages of the 1964 campaign, Lyndon Johnson quieted criticism by joining the American Humane Society. And recall the opprobrium leveled at 2012 GOP presidential candidate Mitt Romney when it was revealed that 30 years earlier he had hauled his Irish setter, Seamus, in a crate atop a station wagon on a 12-hour drive to a family vacation on Lake Huron. With the exception of Lincoln, we mostly hear about politicians’ dogs. But there have been many other cats in our leaders’ lives. Among them was the Clintons’ cat, Socks, a stray who joined them when Bill was governor of Arkansas. Of the bitter relations in the White House between the cat and the family’s labrador, Buddy, Clinton later said, “I had more luck with the Israelis and the Palestinians than I had with Socks and Buddy.” The first defense in a campaign of a pet’s honor, albeit tongue-in-cheek, was by Franklin Roosevelt in his 1944 bid for reelection. Republicans, led by presidential nominee Thomas Dewey, falsely claimed FDR wasted millions of taxpayer dollars transporting his beloved scotch-terrier, Fala, in a Navy destroyer from the Aleutian Islands. Speaking to a Teamsters Union dinner that was nationally broadcast by all the radio networks, FDR complained that Republicans were not content with attacks on him and his family but now they were attacking Fala. Of the attacks, he asserted Fala’s “Scotch soul was furious. He has not been the same dog since. I am accustomed to hearing malicious falsehoods about myself &#8230; But I think I have a right to resent, to object, to libelous statements about my dog.” The first response to his speech was laughter and cheers in the dining hall and throughout the country. The second was an easy reelection for Roosevelt over Dewey. Stealing a bit of Roosevelt’s pet magic, the GOP’s 1952 vice-presidential candidate, Richard Nixon, successfully kept his place on the ticket by using Checkers, a cocker spaniel that a supporter had given to his daughters. Eisenhower was being urged to dump Nixon because he had a legal but unusual campaign expense fund paid by supporters’ donations. Nixon responded to criticism with a nationally televised speech in which he explained the fund’s legality and his own comparatively modest financial circumstances. He concluded by saying that regardless of what his political opponents wanted, he would not return Checkers to his original owner because his “kids, like all kids, love their dog.” The result of his Checkers speech was that Nixon stayed on the ticket and went on to serve eight years as vice president and six as president and was our only president to be forced to resign. Americans want their politicians to be on the side of pets as shown by the success of Roosevelt’s Fala and Nixon’s Checkers speeches. The fabricated Trump/Vance accounts of supposed Haitian illegal immigrants stealing and then eating dogs and cats in Springfield, Ohio, were meant to show the reverse — that the policies of Kamala Harris and her boss, President Joe Biden, were leading to the destruction of America’s much-loved pets. Ken Peterson Of course, Americans quickly learned the Haitians were in the U.S. legally and that there were no stolen or eaten pets. Further, the supposed stolen and eaten cat, Miss Sassy — belonging to a Facebook poster’s “neighbor’s daughter’s friend” whose absence started the whole affair — was found very much alive in her owner’s basement. There are two lessons here. The first is the utter shamelessness of Vance’s contention that his lies are justified since they focus attention on immigration and can thereby help Trump and him get elected. The second, less political and more useful, is that before you complain of a stolen and eaten cat, check your basement. Ken Peterson is a retired attorney who served as the state’s commissioner of labor and industry under Govs. Rudy Perpich and Mark Dayton. He and his family have owned several cats but the only one in his life today is his daughter’s Cornish rex, Pyewacket. Want to add your voice?
