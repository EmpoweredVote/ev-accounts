PEOPLE, NOT THE MACHINE
STEVE HILTON’S PLAN TO BAN ROBO-FIRING AND MAKE WORK MORE HUMAN

Democratic lawmakers and labor unions in California are right about something basic: a machine should not decide whether a worker keeps a job.

Their No Robo Bosses Act, SB 947, is a step in the right direction. The California Chamber has labeled it a “Cost Driver” and helped lead the opposition, arguing that rules around AI-assisted discipline and firing are too burdensome for employers.

What a ridiculous thing for the Chamber to say.

There is no candidate who has been more strongly and consistently committed to improving the business climate in California than Steve Hilton; most of his career has been spent in the private sector, a majority as a small business owner. But the idea that allowing humans to be fired solely by AI is some kind of onerous burden on business is preposterous. The California Chamber just makes a fool of itself - and discredits every business - with this kind of idiotic, knee-jerk truculence.

A job is central to self-worth, and workers should be treated with humanity and dignity. A worker who loses a job deserves to know who made that decision and why. And the answer must never be: “The AI did it. No humans involved.”

Steve would go further than SB 947. The bill would still allow an automated system to help produce a firing or discipline decision, so long as a person later reviews the result. But if a machine has pointed to a worker, scored them, or recommended what should happen to them, it has already helped make the decision.

THE PROBLEM

A San Francisco start-up is running an experiment with an AI-run retail store. The AI recently fired a worker in the store, the first recorded instance of a human being fired entirely by AI. The system had written an employee handbook, then lost track of it. Its developers had to remind it what the handbook said before it could make the decision to fire the employee.

A job is not an entry in a spreadsheet. It is the rent, the grocery bill, the car payment, the mortgage, the kid’s vacations and Christmas presents. Yet more and more employers are using technology to watch workers, measure their productivity, and turn their work into a number. Their movements, keystrokes, sales figures, delivery times, and customer ratings can all be fed into a system that decides who looks like a problem.

Then the worker gets fewer hours, a disciplinary warning, or a form email saying their account has been deactivated. For someone working through an app, deactivation is firing. It means the income a family relies on can disappear overnight, with no real person to call and no way to explain that the system got it wrong.

Big Business sees a cost-saving tool. Steve sees a basic question of fairness: who decided that a particular worker would no longer be able to pay their bills? A human being? Or a machine?

The California Chamber thinks the protections in SB 947 are too burdensome. Steve thinks it is more burdensome to lose your job because a computer got the facts wrong, made a bad prediction, or turned your life into a score.

California should be the best place in the world to build technology, including AI. But it should not become the place where powerful companies use it to make workers disposable.

WHAT STEVE WILL DO
KEEP AI OUT OF FIRING DECISIONS

Steve will prohibit employers from using AI or automated decision systems to determine whether a worker should be fired, disciplined, suspended, demoted, have their regular hours cut, or be deactivated.

It does not matter whether the system makes a recommendation, produces a productivity score, flags a worker, predicts a future problem, or hands a manager a shortlist. If it is used to decide whether someone loses their job or income, it is out.

This is a clear line. Of course software, and AI, can help a business operate and become more productive. It cannot be used to decide who gets to keep a paycheck.

PUT A REAL PERSON ON THE RECORD

If an employer takes someone’s job or income away, a named and accountable person must make the judgment and the decision.

That person must be able to explain the reason, consider the worker’s response, and stand behind the outcome. “The system flagged you” is not an answer.

A bad GPS location, a mistake in a time record, or a missing sales entry should not become a pink slip. Workers must have a fair opportunity to see and correct factual errors before a decision is made.

Companies also cannot use technology as a back door around laws against discrimination. A worker’s disability, pregnancy, health information, use of leave, family responsibilities, or private life should not be turned into material for an employment decision.

PROTECT APP-BASED WORKERS

SB 947’s current version removed independent contractors from the bill. Steve’s rule will not leave app-based workers behind.

If a delivery driver, caregiver, or other worker loses access to the platform that provides their income, that is firing in all but name. The same rule should apply.

MAKE WORK MORE HUMAN

Workers who believe an employer used AI to decide their fate should be able to bring a complaint to the Labor Commissioner. Companies that hide behind an algorithm should face real consequences.

California can lead the world in AI without letting Big Business dehumanize workers. Steve made these arguments in More Human in 2015, and they are more valid now than ever.
